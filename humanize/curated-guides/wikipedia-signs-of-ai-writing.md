> *[Lead Figure Caption on Wikipedia]*: LLMs tend to have an identifiable writing style, as seen in this June 2025 response.

This is a list of writing and formatting conventions typical of [AI chatbots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_chatbot) such as [ChatGPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT), with real examples taken from Wikipedia articles, drafts, comments, and other content. It is a [field guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_guide) to help detect [undisclosed AI-generated content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:LLMDISCLOSE) *on Wikipedia*: while some of the signs may be broadly applicable, some may not apply in a non-Wikipedia context.[a] Not all text featuring these indicators is AI-generated, as the [large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model) that power AI chatbots are trained on human writing, including Wikipedia. Many elements of AI writing can be found in editorials, blogs, or fan fiction.

Moreover, this list is *descriptive*, not *prescriptive*; it consists of observations, not rules. Advice about formatting or language to avoid can be found in the [policies and guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:PAG) and the [Manual of Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:MOS), but does not belong on this page. The patterns listed here are also only potential *signs* of a problem, not *the problem itself*. While many of these issues are immediately obvious and easy to fix—e.g., excessive boldface, broken markup, citation style quirks—they can point to less outwardly visible problems that carry [much more serious policy risks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIFAIL). **Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder.** The actual problems are those deeper concerns, so make sure to address them, either yourself or by flagging them, per the [relevant guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WikiProject_AI_Cleanup/Guide).

The [speedy deletion policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Speedy_deletion) criterion [G15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:G15) (Unambiguously LLM-generated pages) lists some signs of AI writing, but is limited to the most objective ones. The remaining signs covered here are not sufficient on their own for speedy deletion.

As of October 2026, the most common signs of newly AI-generated text on Wikipedia (besides the unambiguous indicators above) are [Markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markdown), [AI edit summaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Edit_summaries), [hedging disclaimers about source availability and usage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Hedging_disclaimers_about_source_availability_and_recommended_usage), [vague expression of connection or association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Vague_expression_of_connection_or_association), and undue emphasis on identification by primary sources.

### AI detection tools

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § AI detection tools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#AI_detection_tools)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIDETECTION`, `WP:AIDETECTOR`

*See also: [Wikipedia:WikiProject AI Cleanup/Guide and resources § LLM detection software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WikiProject_AI_Cleanup/Guide_and_resources#LLM_detection_software), and [Wikipedia:Pangram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Pangram)*

Do not solely rely on [artificial intelligence content detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_content_detection) tools (such as [GPTZero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPTZero) and [Pangram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangram_%28AI_detector%29)). While they perform better than random chance, these tools have non-trivial error rates.[1][2] Detectors can be susceptible to factors such as text modifications (e.g. paraphrasing, markup, and spacing changes) and the use of models not seen during detector training.[3] A high percentage of detected AI writing from the results of a detection tool is **not** a valid criterion for speedy deletion under G15.

Do not rely too much on your own judgment. Humans are notoriously bad at distinguishing human and LLM-generated text. While research on humans' abilities to detect AI-generated text is still limited, a 2025 study has shown that human ability to distinguish LLM text from human is no better than random chance.[4] Another 2025 study on German theses has shown that humans managed a "recognition rate of 57 % for AI texts and 64 % for human-generated texts".[5]

In a 2025 preprint, a subset of 'expert' annotators correctly identified 90% of AI-generated text (and misidentified 3.3% of human-written text) as AI-generated. Study participants who didn't use LLMs much did only slightly better than random chance (in both directions).[1]

Note, also, that human speech and writing is being influenced by LLMs, and thus they are becoming more similar. This was already evident in 2024, as shown by a study that detected a significant LLM influence in spoken content (e.g. conversational podcasts).[6] Further studies seem to confirm this influence on language,[7] including semantics and word choices.[8] However, this is a lagging and patchwork process, and the reverse is not true: LLM output is not becoming more similar to that of human writing.[9]

## Content

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Content)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIWTW`, `WP:AI-ISM`, `WP:LLMISM`

LLMs (and [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network) in general) use statistical algorithms to guess (infer) what should come next based on a large corpus of training material. It thus tends to [regress to the mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_to_the_mean); that is, the result tends toward the most statistically likely result that applies to the widest variety of cases. Most LLM-based tools then undergo another round of adjustment, called [reinforcement learning from human feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement_learning_from_human_feedback), to tailor its output toward the most broadly palatable prose. This can simultaneously be a strength and a "tell" for detecting AI-generated content.

For example, LLMs are usually trained on data from the internet in which famous people are generally described with positive, important-sounding language. Consequently, the LLM tends to omit specific, unusual, nuanced facts (which are statistically rare) and replace them with more generic, positive descriptions (which are statistically common). Thus the highly specific "inventor of the first train-coupling device" might become "a revolutionary titan of industry". It is like shouting louder and louder that a portrait shows a uniquely important person, while the portrait itself is fading from a sharp photograph into a blurry, generic sketch. The subject becomes simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated.[b]

This statistical regression to the mean, a smoothing over of specific facts into generic statements, that could equally apply to many topics, makes AI-generated content easier to detect.

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Undue emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Undue_emphasis_on_notability%2C_attribution%2C_and_media_coverage)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:OVERATTRIBUTION`, `WP:AIATTR`

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Citing sources § In-text attribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#In-text_attribution).*

> **Words to watch:** ***independent coverage*, *local/regional/national/*[country name]* media outlets*, *music/business/tech outlets*, *trade publications*, *cited/featured/profiled in***

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Vague expression of connection or association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Vague_expression_of_connection_or_association)

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch § Unsupported attributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions).*

### "Awards and recognition" section

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § "Awards and recognition" section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#%22Awards_and_recognition%22_section)

Section or subsection headers in the format of "*X* and *Y*" are common in (but not exclusive to) AI-generated articles. An "Awards and recognition" section is particularly common (indeed, also simply a "Recognition" section). This is a manifestation of AI-generated text's undue and particular focus on [legacy and trends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AILEGACY) and its tendency to vaguely allude to ["coverage" or "recognition" of the subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIATTR) by various outlets.

While human editors may also include sections about a subject's legacy or impact on the world or their industry, the specific wording of "Awards and recognition" is nearly ubiquitous in AI generated articles, and much more common than in human writing even when comparing against [articles tagged as containing promotional content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Articles_with_a_promotional_tone).

## Language and grammar

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Language and grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Language_and_grammar)

AI-generated text displays consistent patterns in syntax, word choice, and sentence construction that human writing does not display to nearly the same degree. Conversely, it often struggles to match some syntactic and linguistic patterns characteristic of human writing. Some LLMs deviate more from human writing than others; for example, GPT-4o, the language model used by ChatGPT from May 2024 to August 2025, produces output with more variation from human writing than other contemporaneous language models.[13] Since these are linguistic patterns, they occur consistently regardless of the subject matter, which often gives AI-generated text an identifiable "voice".

### High density of "AI vocabulary" words

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § High density of "AI vocabulary" words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#High_density_of_%22AI_vocabulary%22_words)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIVOCAB`, `WP:AIWORDS`, `WP:AILEXICON`

> **Words to watch:** ***Additionally* (especially beginning a sentence),[14][15] *align with*,[12][16] *boasts* (meaning "has"),[17] *bolstered*,[16] *crucial*,[1][14][15] *deep dive*,[18] *delve*,[12][16][1][19] *emphasizing*,[12][16] *enduring*,[16] *enhance*,[16][1] *fostering*,[16][1] *garner*,[12][16] *highlight* (as a verb),[1][19] *interplay*,[16] *intricate/intricacies*,[12][16][13][19] *landscape* (as an abstract noun),[1] *meticulous/meticulously*,[17][16] *pivotal*,[16][1] *robust*, *showcase*,[12][16][19] *tapestry* (as an abstract noun),[1][13][19] *testament*,[1] *underscore* (as a verb),[12][16][1][19] *valuable*,[14] *vibrant*[13]**

Many studies have demonstrated that LLMs overuse specific words. These words started appearing far more frequently in text produced after 2022, when LLM chatbots became widely accessible.[12][16] They often co-occur in LLM output: where there is one, there are likely others.[20] While most of these studies have analyzed scientific abstracts or fiction, "AI vocabulary" words are also ubiquitous in LLM-based encyclopedias, such as [Grokipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grokipedia), and in AI-generated Wikipedia text. One or two of these words appearing in an edit may be coincidental, but an edit (post-2022) introducing lots of them, lots of times, is one of the strongest tells for AI use.

The words that LLMs overuse have changed over time. For instance, the word *[delve](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/delve)* was famously overused by ChatGPT in 2023 and early 2024, but became less frequent later in 2024, then dropped off sharply in 2025.[21][14] Below is a breakdown of which words frequently recur together during which LLM "era". While these are not hard cutoffs, they should give you a rough idea of how "earlier" vs "later" LLM output reads.

- **2023 to mid-2024** (GPT-4): *Additionally*, *boasts*, *bolstered*, *crucial*, *delve*, *emphasizing*, *enduring*, *garner*, *intricate/intricacies*, *interplay*, *key*, *landscape*, *meticulously*, *pivotal*, *underscore*, *tapestry*, *testament*, *valuable*, *vibrant*
- **Mid-2024 to mid-2025** (GPT-4o): *align with*, *bolstered*, *crucial*, *emphasizing*, *enhance*, *enduring*, *fostering*, *highlighting*, *pivotal*, *showcasing*, *underscore*, *vibrant*
- **Mid-2025 to mid-2026** (GPT-5): *emphasizing*, *enhance*, *highlighting*, *showcasing* (plus words associated with ["Canned emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Canned_emphasis_on_notability,_attribution,_and_media_coverage))
- **Mid-2026 and on** (GPT-6): *challenge*, *clearer*, *dependable*, *echoed*, *foster*, *leverage*, *matters*, *multifaceted*, *practical*, *prioritize*, *quietly*, *steady*, *universally* (with much more [period and comma usage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Em-dash) and shorter sentences around a strict 8–20 word limit)[22]

The distribution of "AI vocabulary" is also somewhat different depending on the chatbot or LLM used.[13] Grok output is particularly idiosyncratic: it overuses superficially "scientific" words like *causal*, *empirical*, *correlate*, and continues to overuse *underscore* as of 2026.

This section is to be taken as literally as possible: a word being overused by AI does *not* imply that its synonyms are also overused. Also, keep context in mind. For example, while the figurative use of "underscore" is ubiquitous in earlier AI text, the word can also refer to a literal underline mark or to [incidental music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidental_music).

> *Wikipedia Notice:* There are no examples given for 2026 AI vocabulary. Recent examples should be given.

### Avoidance of basic copulatives ("is"/"are" phrases)

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Avoidance of basic copulatives ("is"/"are" phrases)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Avoidance_of_basic_copulatives_%28%22is%22%2F%22are%22_phrases%29)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AINOCOPULA`, `WP:AIREPRESENTS`

> **Words to watch:** ***serves as/stands as/marks/functions as/operates as/represents [a]*, *boasts/features/maintains/offers [a]*, *refers to***

> *Wikipedia Notice:* The examples given in this section are **outdated** and may not reflect the behaviour of current models. More recent examples should be added. If no recent examples can be found, this sign should be moved to [§ Historical indicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Historical_indicators).

LLM-generated text often replaces simple constructions that use [copulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copula_%28linguistics%29) such as *is* or *are* with constructions such as *serves as a* or *mark the*. This pattern has been observed in GPT and Gemini models.[15] One study documented an over 10% decrease in the usage of the words *is* and *are* in academic writing in 2023, with no major changes in their frequency before that.[23] Similarly, LLMs prefer to use [marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:BUZZ)-related verbs like *features*, *offers*, and the like to their neutral synonym *has*. (Note: Do not confuse this with *has* used in the [present perfect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_perfect) form, as in *has been featured*.) Sometimes—especially in more recent AI output—these constructions are more elaborate and link to [vague expressions of connection or association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AICONNECT) between two topics, e.g., *ventured into politics as a candidate* versus *was a candidate*, or *began his career as* versus *was*.

A similar decline in "is"/"are" constructions has been observed on Wikipedia, especially when controlling for lead paragraphs (which usually follow a formulaic structure of "[article subject] is...]" and thus skew the data).[15] It is particularly visible in AI copyedits, which will often "improve" text in this way. The study above also demonstrated that when GPT-3.5 was prompted to "Revise the following sentence" in 10,000 abstracts, the words *is* and *are* appeared less often in the revised versions.[23]

In lead sentences, LLMs will sometimes avoid *is* by writing *refers to* [as though the article were about the word or term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Leads_treating_Wikipedia_lists_or_broad_article_titles_as_proper_nouns) instead of the subject directly.

### Negative parallelisms

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Negative parallelisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Negative_parallelisms)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIPARALLEL`

*See also: [Negative parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_parallelism)*

When LLMs describe a subject, their output may seem as though it is clearing up a common misconception, or as though the audience may be reaching an incomplete or incorrect conclusion about that subject. This kind of contrast can come across as trying to retroactively challenge such thinking by pointing out another characteristic that the subject may possess alongside (or in the place of) one or more previously mentioned characteristics. While it is common among human writers (especially in "common misconceptions" or "myths busted" [listicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listicle)), it is stereotypically an "AI sign".

## Style

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Style)

*Further information: [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing/Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style)*

AI-generated text tends to exhibit some stylistic features that are not expected to be found as often in human-made articles. These include:

- Using [title case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AITITLECASE) instead of [sentence case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_case) in section titles.
- [Overuse of boldface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIBOLD) in prose, sometimes [misusing asterisks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIASTERISK) in an attempt to do so.
- Frequently presenting information in [inline-header vertical lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIBOLD).
- Using the incorrect heading levels. For example, an LLM might [start an article with a level 1 heading containing the article title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style#Title_heading), or [use multiple level 1 headings throughout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style#Overuse_of_level_1_headings).
- Affixing [emoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIEMOJI) at the beginning of section titles or list entries.
- Using [curly quotation marks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AICURLY) (“”) and apostrophes (’) instead of straight ones.

#### Mid-2026–present

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Mid-2026–present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Mid-2026%E2%80%93present)

As of mid-2026, chatbots frequently include disclaimers about how sources should be used or other "corrective framings."[9] There are at least three known patterns:

- [Negative parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Y_rather_than_X) constructions, usually with "rather than", warning that a source or claim should be treated as [something speculative/subjective/symbolic] rather than [something definitive/verified].
- Cautioning that the available information does not establish [some firm conclusion].
- Disambiguating a topic by stating it "should not be confused" with another topic. (Note: This pattern is less reliable than the above two.)

## Markup irregularities

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Markup irregularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Markup_irregularities)

*Further information: [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing/Markup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup)*

AI chatbots prompted to write content for Wikipedia tend to make errors that wouldn't be expected to appear often in human-written text. These include:

- Using [Markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markdown) (which often manifests as attempting to use [asterisks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIASTERISK) for bold text, [number signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIHASHTAG) to denote section headings), using `---` or `----` to denote section dividers, attempting Markdown-style link formatting `[url](text)` instead of wikilink formatting, and most unambiguous of all, rendering text as Markdown code blocks by wrapping it in triple backticks `` (```) ``, often prefaced with "wikitext".
- A chatbot failing to properly convert citations to text and [exposing its internal data structure or styling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:OAICITE) to the reader. This is an unambiguous indicator, and every chatbot has a different pattern; for example, ChatGPT sometimes renders markup as `contentReference[oaicite:1]`. More examples can be seen in the above link.
- Other bugs: Placing articles in [nonexistent categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIREDCAT) and attempting to transclude [nonexistent templates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup#Non-existent_templates).

## Citations

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Citations)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIFICTREF`

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Fictitious references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Fictitious_references).*

*See also: [Template:Failed verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template:Failed_verification)*

### Broken external links

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Broken external links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Broken_external_links)

If a new article or draft has multiple citations with external links, and **several** of them are broken (e.g., non-existent websites or [404 errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/404_error)), this is a strong sign of an AI-generated page, particularly if the dead links are not found in website archiving sites like the [Internet Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Archive). Most links [become broken over time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_rot), but these factors make it unlikely that the link was ever real.

Watch out for: Links that don't work for you, but do work for other people (e.g., journal articles accessed through a university library); links that were mangled by bots and scripts (e.g., to add incorrect identifiers or to remove seemingly unnecessary parts of the URL); links that are missing the start or end (a sign of a human copy/pasting the URL).

### Invalid DOI and ISBNs

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Invalid DOI and ISBNs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Invalid_DOI_and_ISBNs)

*For non-AI-specific guidance about DOI and ISBNs, see [Wikipedia:DOI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:DOI) and [Wikipedia:ISBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:ISBN).*

A [checksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checksum) can be used to verify [ISBNs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBN). An invalid checksum is a very likely sign that an ISBN is incorrect, and citation templates display [a warning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:CS1_errors#bad_isbn) if so. Similarly, [DOIs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_object_identifier) are more resistant to link rot than regular hyperlinks. Unresolvable DOIs and invalid ISBNs can be indicators of [hallucinated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_%28AI%29) references.

### DOIs that lead to unrelated articles

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § DOIs that lead to unrelated articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#DOIs_that_lead_to_unrelated_articles)

An LLM may generate references to non-existent scholarly articles with DOIs that appear valid but are, in reality, assigned to unrelated articles. Example passage generated by ChatGPT:

> Ohm’s Law applies to many materials and components that are "ohmic," meaning their resistance remains constant regardless of the applied voltage or current. However, it does not hold for non-linear devices like diodes or transistors [1][2].
>
> 1. M. E. Van Valkenburg, “The validity and limitations of Ohm’s law in non-linear circuits,” Proceedings of the IEEE, vol. 62, no. 6, pp. 769–770, Jun. 1974. [doi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doi_%28identifier%29):[10.1109/PROC.1974.9547](https://doi.org/10.1109%2FPROC.1974.9547)
>
> 2. C. L. Fortescue, “Ohm’s Law in alternating current circuits,” Proceedings of the IEEE, vol. 55, no. 11, pp. 1934–1936, Nov. 1967. [doi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doi_%28identifier%29):[10.1109/PROC.1967.6033](https://doi.org/10.1109%2FPROC.1967.6033)

Both *Proceedings of the IEEE* citations are completely made up. The DOIs lead to different citations and have other problems as well. For instance, [C. L. Fortescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_LeGeyt_Fortescue) was dead for 30+ years at the purported time of writing, and [Vol 55, Issue 11](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/xpl/tocresult.jsp?isnumber=31102&punumber=5) does not list any articles that match anything remotely close to the information given in reference 2.

Note: From 2018 to 2023, [a UX issue in VisualEditor](https://phabricator.wikimedia.org/T198456) led many editors to accidentally insert references to [PubMed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PubMed) articles with low ID numbers (PMIDs), resulting in obviously irrelevant citations like an article about rat livers (PMID 9) being cited in [List of Disney television films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Diff/972043377) in 2020. These may resemble AI hallucinations (and should be fixed regardless), but they generally are not.

### Book citations without page numbers or URLs

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Book citations without page numbers or URLs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Book_citations_without_page_numbers_or_URLs)

LLMs often generate book citations that do not include page numbers. This passage, for example, was generated by ChatGPT:

> Ohm's Law is a fundamental principle in the field of electrical engineering and physics that states the current passing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points, provided the temperature remains constant. Mathematically, it is expressed as V=R, where V is the voltage, I is the current, and R is the resistance. The law was formulated by German physicist Georg Simon Ohm in 1827, and it serves as a cornerstone in the analysis and design of electrical circuits [1].
>
> 1. Dorf, R. C., & Svoboda, J. A. (2010). Introduction to Electric Circuits (8th ed.). Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons. [ISBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBN_%28identifier%29) [9780470521571](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:BookSources/9780470521571).

The book reference appears valid – a book on electric circuits would likely have information about Ohm's law – but without the page number, that citation is not useful for verifying the claims in the prose.

Some LLM-generated book citations include page numbers, and the book exists, but the cited pages do not verify the text. Signs to look out for: the book is on a somewhat general topic or frequently referenced in its field, and the citation does not include a URL (not mandatory for book citations, but editors creating legitimate book citations often include a link to [an online version of the text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Book_sources#Online_text)). Example:

> Analysts note that traditionalists often appeal to prudence, stability, and Edmund Burke’s notion of “prescription,” while reactionaries invoke moral urgency and cultural emergency, framing the present as a deviation from an idealized past. [1]
>
> 1. Goldwater, Barry (1960). The Conscience of a Conservative. Victor Publishing. p. 12.

This may look like a reasonable citation, but searching an [online version of the book for "Burke" produces no results](https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_Conscience_of_a_Conservative/PKd_EQAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=burke).

### Incorrect or unconventional use of references

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Incorrect or unconventional use of references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Incorrect_or_unconventional_use_of_references)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AICITESTYLE`

## Comment-specific indicators

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Comment-specific indicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Comment-specific_indicators)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AICOMMENT`

*Further information: [Wikipedia:Signs of AI use in comments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_use_in_comments)*

*See also: [Wikipedia:Identifying LLM unblock requests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Identifying_LLM_unblock_requests) and [Wikipedia:The LLM-written ANI report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:The_LLM-written_ANI_report)*

*"WP:AICOMMENTS" redirects here. For hidden creator-facing comments in AI-generated articles, see [§ Collaborative communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Collaborative_communication).*

In many cases, some users have copy-pasted text from AI chatbots into their comments. Comments and nominations suspected of having been pasted from an LLM may be collapsed via {{[collapse AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template:Collapse_AI)}} or closed entirely in accordance with [Wikipedia:Talk page guidelines § LLM-generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Talk_page_guidelines#LLM-generated). Asides from making any of the mistakes listed on this page, editors who use large language models or similar technology to write their comments are also likely to:

- [Misquote policies and guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIFICTPOLICY) and [cite made-up shortcuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AISHORTCUT) that don't lead to any existing project page. In rare cases, they might simply [cite "WP"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AISHORTCUTFAIL).
- [Transclude maintenance banners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AITRANSCLUSION) whenever they mention them.
- Post lengthy comments [divided into sections with titles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AISECTION), either written in [Markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:MARKDOWN), plain text, or as [level-2 or level-3 subheadings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AISUBHEADING).
- Downplay their LLM use by [claiming to have put some human effort into their work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIMADEBUTSTILLOK) or that [their comments reflect their thoughts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AICOMMENTSAREMINE).
- [Request input from their critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIWANTFEEDBACK) or [other editors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIREVIEW) to help them determine [exactly what needs to be improved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WHERESTHEAI).
- Dismiss concerns about the origin of their content as being based on [unsubstantiated speculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIUNPROVEN) rather than "[concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:CONCRETE)" facts or evidence.
- Urge critics to [redirect their concerns towards improving content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:LETSFOCUSON) instead of worrying about whether it was produced by or modified via a large language model.

Many LLM-generated comments tend to sound like they're coming from a [clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerk) or [customer service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_service) representative, with users claiming to have "carefully reviewed" their supposed work, [apologizing for any inconvenience they "may have" caused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AITALKSIGNS#Apologies_for_potential_past_damage), and saying things along the lines of "If there are specific issues that need to be resolved, please point them out to me, and I will try to resolve them." Given that AI-powered writing assistance tools are often marketed for use at work, this may not be surprising.

## Edit summaries

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Edit summaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Edit_summaries)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AISUMMARY`, `WP:AISUMM`

AI [edit summaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Edit_summary) tend to follow "templates" with distinct idiosyncracies that are uncommon to nonexistent in edit summaries prior to 2023. The presence of such summaries strongly suggests that the edits themselves are also AI-generated, as it is unlikely someone would use AI for a simple summary but not the much more time-consuming task of writing.

Note that these are *rigid formulae*. The more a summary deviates from the pattern, the less likely it is to be AI-generated. Edit summaries with [common abbreviations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Edit_summary_legend), such as "ce" for "copy edit", are also unlikely to be wholly AI-generated.

As with all LLM-generated text, typical AI edit summaries have changed over time. Older AI edit summaries are often written in first-person with typical signs of older LLM output, such as [AI vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIVOCAB), [emoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIEMOJI), (attempted) [list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AILIST) formatting, or [markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:MARKDOWN) formatting.[f] Newer AI summaries are more likely to be exhaustively procedural about the editing process and how it complies with Wikipedia guidelines and policies. Here are some representative examples from various years.

### Canned assurance of adherence to Wikipedia policies and guidelines

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Canned assurance of adherence to Wikipedia policies and guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Canned_assurance_of_adherence_to_Wikipedia_policies_and_guidelines)

> **Words to watch:** **ensured that... adheres to, refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, improved, in compliance/complies with, Wikipedia [guidelines/style/standards], revised, verifiability, neutrality, neutral tone, encyclopedic tone, clarity, flow**

AI-generated edit summaries will often [reassure the reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIASSURE) to a nearly self-conscious extent that the edit was made in compliance with various Wikipedia PAGs like neutrality and sourcing requirements. They will often directly state that these are Wikipedia's guidelines.

Human editors do occasionally express that they are editing text because it violates a particular manual of style entry or guideline, but this is normally done in a brief and specific manner with a link to the particular item. For example, "removed excessive links per [MOS:OVERLINK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS:OVERLINK)", whereas the AI-generated equivalent is usually more verbose and yet less specific (because the human editor behind the bot does not know the relevant guidelines to be able to prompt anything more specific than "make this more neutral"), explaining that they reorganised a section to "[ensure neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AINPOV)", "improve attribution", "comply with the manual of style" or the like. Human editors are also unlikely to spell out the obvious fact that edits they make on Wikipedia are *for* Wikipedia.

The more of these assurances there are stacked in a single edit summary, especially if they cover a wide variety of "improvements" that a human editor is less likely to cover in a single edit, the stronger the sign.

> Revised the Capabilities section for <mark>clarity and compliance with the Manual of Style;</mark> removed unnecessary language parameters, consolidated overlapping fine-tuning sections, <mark>corrected promotional wording,</mark> and distinguished preprints from established findings.
>
> — Edit summary from [this July 2026 revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Diff/1363764012) to [GPT-4o](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-4o)

> Updated the feature-film section with current release information, added coverage of David and Solo Mio, corrected Sound of Freedom details, and <mark>improved sourcing and neutrality.</mark>
>
> — Edit summary from [this July 2026 revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Diff/1363721990) to [Angel Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_Studios)

> Reorganize and copyedit <mark>for a more neutral tone.</mark> Condensed lists. <mark>Removed promotional</mark> and mostly unrelated material. <mark>Improve citations</mark> with museums, grant-organization, independent arts sources, etc. Added 2026 SOLA Award.
>
> — Edit summary from [this July 2026 revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Diff/1363709326) to [Carol Milne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Milne)

### Specific mentions of "preserved" or "retained" information, "avoided" mistakes, and other procedural statements

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Specific mentions of "preserved" or "retained" information, "avoided" mistakes, and other procedural statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Specific_mentions_of_%22preserved%22_or_%22retained%22_information%2C_%22avoided%22_mistakes%2C_and_other_procedural_statements)

> **Words to watch:** **preserved/preserving, retained/retaining, avoided/avoiding, ensured/ensuring, aimed/aiming to**

It is unusual for a human edit summary to include mention of material that was not edited or things that they didn't do. It is, however, exactly what one might expect from an AI which has been prompted to edit the article to accomplish *X* and *Y* but to specifically preserve or avoid changing *Z*.

As seen in the examples below, this typically goes hand in hand with the vague, canned assurances of adherence to/improvement in line with the PAGs/the MOS also discussed in this section; in the format of "Revised [section] to [improve/ensure/etc] [neutrality/verifiability/etc] while preserving [whatever]" or the inverse.

### Overemphasis on presence/reliability of citations

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Overemphasis on presence/reliability of citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Overemphasis_on_presence%2Freliability_of_citations)

> **Words to watch:** **added sourced [information/content/infobox/section], added verified [information/content/infobox/section], added [coverage/citations/references], improved attribution, with [independent/secondary/third-party/peer-reviewed] sources**

One of the common general signs of AI generated text is the tendency to [allude to the existence of coverage but not actually summarise the content of that coverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIATTR). A similar form of this manifests in edit summaries, where an excessive focus is placed on the fact that the content added is "sourced", "closely attributed" to "reliable sources", that "citations" or "coverage" were added, and so on. They will often note that they are independent or secondary sources, echoing the guidelines on notability or reliability.

A human editor is far more likely to focus on the actual content of the prose they added (e.g., "Added info about the artist's debut") rather than only vaguely mentioning the source that information is linked to.

> Build out Professional associations, Regulatory status, and Scope of practice sections <mark>with sourced content;</mark> add Exercise Physiology row to comparison table <mark>with ESSA citations</mark>
>
> — Edit summary from [this July 2026 revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Diff/1363765282) to [Myotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myotherapy)

> Expanded the article with new sections, <mark>added sourced information, references,</mark> and internal/external links.
>
> — Edit summary from [this July 2026 revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Diff/1363991030) to [Alexandr Bilinkis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandr_Bilinkis)

> Add <mark>sourced Military culture section</mark> covering samurai households,literature, ritual, religion, firearms, naval traditions, martial training, medicine, games and Meiji-era bushidō.
>

## Miscellaneous

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Miscellaneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Miscellaneous)

### Pronounced shift in writing style

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Pronounced shift in writing style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Pronounced_shift_in_writing_style)

A sudden shift in an editor's writing style, such as unexpectedly flawless grammar compared to their other communication (e.g., talk page comments versus text added), may indicate the use of AI. This is especially likely if that other writing predates November 2022. More subtly, if an editor has used AI for several years, the writing style of their edits will often change in parallel with contemporaneous AI tools: their 2023 edits will resemble 2023 LLM output, their 2025 edits will resemble 2025 LLM output, and so on.

The reverse also applies: If a user has edits that predate LLM chatbots, and their writing style has remained consistent between those older edits and their current ones (e.g., frequent use of boldface, list formatting, etc.), that suggests the newer edits are less likely to be AI.

### Permissions gaming

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Permissions gaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Permissions_gaming)

[Permissions gaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:PGAME) is a form of disruptive editing where someone makes many benign-seeming but unconstructive edits, often on disparate topics or in quick succession, until their edit count is high enough to raise their [user access level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:User_access_levels), allowing them to pursue their real goal of adding spam, vandalism, or contentious content.

Because AI chatbots are good at generating a lot of benign-looking content very quickly, people who game permissions after 2023 often do so with throwaway AI rewrites or additions to dozens of unrelated articles. If no one goes back and cleans up their edits, the result is a large swath of undetected and unreviewed AI content.

Note: This sign should only be used in one direction. Someone rapidly adding a lot of AI-generated text is *not* necessarily permissions gaming and should not be accused of it barring other evidence. However, if someone *is* found or reasonably suspected to be permissions gaming, and has done so by rapidly adding or changing a lot of text, those edits may be AI.

### Differences between LLMs

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Differences between LLMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Differences_between_LLMs)

Each model and version of AI chatbots have a distinctive way of writing ([idiolect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiolect)),[27][11] and what is typical for GPT-5 is not necessarily characteristic of GPT-4 or Gemini.

For example, text from ChatGPT (circa GPT-4o-2024-08-06) and Grok (Grok-Beta, as of late 2024/early 2025) exhibit characteristics that Gemini (1-5 Pro) and Claude (3.5-Sonnet) do not:

- [focusing on broader context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AILEGACY) is more characteristic of ChatGPT and Grok than Gemini and Claude.[11]
- Gemini and Claude responses tend to be more concise than responses from ChatGPT and Grok.[g][11]

ChatGPT is historically the most widely used chatbot for Wikipedia edits.[28]

### Biases in content

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Biases in content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Biases_in_content)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIBIAS`, `WP:LLMBIAS`

Bias occurs in LLMs based upon what the LLM was trained to do or the training data the LLM trained upon. One reason LLMs are biased is their training data (e.g. an LLM's bias being skewed towards occidental countries due to training on data primarily originating from western sources). Many other factors play role, such as the manner the LLM model was designed in, or their systems of prioritization.[29] Another factor may be AI hallucinations from less knowledge of a lesser-spoken and used vernacular (such as the Azerbaijani language).[30]

## Signs of human writing

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Signs of human writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Signs_of_human_writing)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:HUMANSIGNS`

### Age of text relative to ChatGPT launch

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Age of text relative to ChatGPT launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Age_of_text_relative_to_ChatGPT_launch)

ChatGPT was launched to the public on November 30, 2022. Although OpenAI had similarly powerful LLMs before then, they were paid services and not easily accessible or known to lay people. Thus, if an edit was made **before November 30, 2022**, AI use can be safely ruled out for the corresponding text. While some older writing displays some of the AI signs given in this list, the vastness of Wikipedia allows for these coincidences.

The age of any given block of text can be found using [Who Wrote That?](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Who_Wrote_That%3F) (WWT) or [WikiBlame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WikiBlame) (WB), tools that find the revision at which the text was inserted. If the text is older than the first revision examined by the tool, WWT will simply display no information for the selected text, while WB will indicate that it was present in the earliest selected revision.

### Ability to explain one's own editorial choices

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Ability to explain one's own editorial choices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Ability_to_explain_one%27s_own_editorial_choices)

Editors should be able to explain why they made an edit or mistake. For example, if an editor inserts a URL that appears fabricated, you can ask how the mix-up occurred instead of jumping to conclusions. If they can supply the correct link and explain it as a human error (perhaps a typo), or share the relevant passage from the real source, that points to an ordinary human error.

### Syntax

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Syntax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Syntax)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AISYNTAX`, `WP:LLMSYNTAX`

LLMs writing or editing Wikipedia articles will attempt, by default, to produce text in what it considers to be "formal, neutral, encyclopedic tone". This manifests as AI-generated text avoiding certain syntactic constructions that are both common in human writing and often perfectly acceptable per the [Manual of Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style) – and in some cases, even preferred by it.

Specifically, the following have been empirically observed, over 25 years of Wikipedia writing, to be more common in Wikipedia articles written by humans than in AI-generated text:

- Simple is/has phrases,[23] such as *there is a*, *it has a*.
- Words with complex, stiff or euphemistic synonyms, such as *wrote* (versus *authored*), *moved* (versus *relocated*), *used* (versus *utilized*), *tried* (versus *attempted*), *died* (versus *passed away*).
- Superlative or definitive statements, such as *one of the best*, *is the only*, *was the first*
- Qualifiers and intensifiers,[31] such as *very*, *perhaps*, *tends to*.
- Isolated wordy constructions such as *as a result of*, *in order to*, *all of the*, *a part of*, or *the fact that*.

## Ineffective indicators

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Ineffective indicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Ineffective_indicators)

False accusations of AI use can [drive away new editors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:BITE) and foster an atmosphere of suspicion. Before claiming AI was used, consider whether the [Dunning–Kruger effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger_effect) or [confirmation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias) may be clouding your judgement. Detecting LLM texts on the basis of style alone is *not* as easy as it seems, see [§ Your detection ability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Your_detection_ability) in this page. Here are several somewhat commonly used indicators that are ineffective in LLM detection—and may even indicate the opposite.

- **Perfect grammar** – While modern LLMs are known for high grammatical proficiency, many editors are also skilled writers or come from professional writing backgrounds. (See also [§ Sudden shift in English variety use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Sudden_shift_in_English_variety_use).)
- **Combination of casual and formal registers, or language that sounds both "clinical" and "emotional"** – This may indicate the casual writing of a person in a technical field, such as computer science. It may also indicate youth, a preference for mixed registers, playfulness, or neurodivergence. In the case of a wiki, it may simply be the result of multiple editors adding to a page.
- **"Bland" or "robotic" prose** – LLM output has specific traits, as detailed above, and it skews positive and verbose by default. While these tendencies are formulaic, they may not scan as "robotic" to those unfamiliar with AI writing.[32]
- **"Fancy", "academic", or "formal" prose** – While LLMs disproportionately favor certain words and phrases, many of which are longer and have more difficult [readability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Readability) scores than some of their synonyms, these are *specific words*. The correlation does not extend to all formal, academic, or "fancy"-sounding prose.[1]
- **Transition words (in isolation)** – Older AI text tended to formulaically overuse certain [transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_%28linguistics%29) like *Additionally,* *Consequently,* and *Notably,* often to begin sentences. However, only a few transition words and phrases are known to be overused by AI in this way. This pattern also has precedence in essay-like writing by humans and is accepted by many style guides, so this is not a strong tell.
- **Unsourced content** – [More than 570,000 articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:All_articles_with_unsourced_statements) are tagged as needing citations, and most of them predate LLMs. Meanwhile, since modern LLM chatbots can search the web and view sources a user provides to it, citations are fairly common now in AI-generated text. This does not mean they are *accurate* citations, but they are there.
- **Bizarre [wikitext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Wikitext)** – While LLMs may hallucinate templates or generate wikitext code with invalid syntax for reasons explained in [§ Use of Markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Use_of_Markdown), they are not likely to generate content with certain random-seeming, "inexplicable" errors and artifacts (excluding the ones listed here in [WP:AIMARKDOWN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AIMARKDOWN)). Bizarrely placed [HTML tags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML_tags) like `<span>` are more indicative of poorly programmed browser extensions or a known bug with Wikipedia's content translation tool ([T113137](https://phabricator.wikimedia.org/T113137)). Misplaced syntax like `''Catch-22 i''s a satirical novel.` (rendered as "*Catch-22 i*s a satirical novel.") are more indicative of mistakes in [VisualEditor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:VisualEditor), where such errors are harder to notice than in [source editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Source_editing).
- **Correct wikitext** – Especially if the person is using the visual editor or has found the [Preview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Show_preview) button, getting the formatting correct, even for complex templates, is normal.

## Historical indicators

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Historical indicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Historical_indicators)

The following indicators were common in text generated by older AI models, but are much less frequent in newer models. They may still be useful for finding older undetected AI-generated edits. Dates are approximate.

### Promotional and advertisement-like language (2023–2025)

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Promotional and advertisement-like language (2023–2025)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Promotional_and_advertisement-like_language_%282023%E2%80%932025%29)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIPUFFERY`, `WP:AIPEACOCK`

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch § Puffery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Puffery).*

*See also: [Wikipedia:Marketing buzzspeak § Marketing buzzspeak and artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Marketing_buzzspeak#Marketing_buzzspeak_and_artificial_intelligence)*

> **Words to watch:** ***boasts a*, *vibrant*, *rich*, *profound*, *enhancing*, *showcasing*, *exemplifies*, *commitment to*, *natural beauty*, *nestled*, *in the heart of*, *groundbreaking*, *renowned*, *featuring*, *diverse array*, ...**

Older LLMs had serious problems keeping a neutral tone. Even when prompted to use an encyclopedic style, their output often tended toward press release-like writing, or like the prose of a travel guide.[33] While this did not apply to all promotional writing -- in particular, AI-generated articles tended to avoid superlative or obviously positive statements like "the best" -- the stylistic trend was noticeable.

However, this blatant promotional tone became less common in AI output starting around 2024[34], and is no longer common in newer LLM output unless a user specifically asks for it. While LLM-generated Wikipedia articles are often "promotional" in terms of [conflict of interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:COI) or the information it includes (e.g., [focusing on awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#%22Awards_and_recognition%22_section)), the prose itself is less likely to be.

### Statements about knowledge cutoffs (2022–2023)

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Statements about knowledge cutoffs (2022–2023)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Statements_about_knowledge_cutoffs_%282022%E2%80%932023%29)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AICUTOFF`

> **Words to watch:** ***Up to my last training update*, *as of my last knowledge update* ...**

A knowledge-cutoff disclaimer is a statement used by an AI chatbot to indicate that the information provided may be incomplete, inaccurate, or outdated.

Older LLMs with fixed [knowledge cutoffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_cutoff), such as [GPT-3.5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-3.5) or [GPT-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-4), were unable to provide any information on events or developments past a certain date (usually the model's last training update).[35] Such LLMs would often remind the user about this by outputting a disclaimer that the information in its response is accurate only up to a certain date, sometimes explicitly mentioning the knowledge cutoff.

**Examples**

> <mark>As of my last knowledge update in January 2022</mark>, I don't have specific information about the current status or developments related to the "Chester Mental Health Center" in today's era.

### Section summaries

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Section summaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Section_summaries)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:CONCLUSION`, `WP:INCONCLUSION`

> **Words to watch:** ***In summary*, *In conclusion*, *Overall* ...**

When generating longer outputs (such as when told to "write an article"), older LLMs often added sections titled "Conclusion" or similar, and often ended paragraphs or sections by summarizing and restating its core idea.[36] Many of these older models are still used because of lower or absent token costs, so this sign is still observed after 2024, just much less.

### Prompt refusal

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Prompt refusal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Prompt_refusal)

> **Words to watch:** ***as an AI language model*, *as a large language model*, *I cannot offer medical advice, but I can...*, *I'm sorry* ...**

In the past, AI chatbots occasionally declined to answer prompts as written, usually with apologies and reminders that they are AI language models. Attempting to be helpful, chatbots often gave suggestions or answers to alternative, similar requests. Outright refusals have become increasingly rare.

**Examples**

> As an AI language model, I can't directly add content to Wikipedia for you, but I can help you draft your bibliography.

### Overuse of em dashes (2022–September 2026)

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Overuse of em dashes (2022–September 2026)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Overuse_of_em_dashes_%282022%E2%80%93September_2026%29)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIDASH`

*For non-AI-specific guidance about the use of dashes, see [Wikipedia:Manual of Style § Dashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style#Dashes).*

While human editors and writers often use [em dashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Em_dash) (—), LLM output is much more disposed to use them than nonprofessional human-written text of the same genre, and uses them in places where humans are more likely to use commas, parentheses, colons, or (misused) hyphens (-) and [en dashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/En_dash) (–). LLMs especially tend to use em dashes in a formulaic, pat way, often mimicking "punched up" sales-like writing by over-emphasizing clauses or parallelisms.[21][19] AI-generated em dashes are usually surrounded by spaces, contrary to common typographic guidelines (which most human users of em dashes will be familiar with).

This sign is most useful when taken in combination with other indicators, not by itself. It is much more common on discussion pages than in article text. Also, because LLMs' use of em-dashes has become somewhat notorious, some AI companies have attempted to make their newer chatbots suppress their use, most notably OpenAI's [GPT-5.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-5.1).[37] A July 2026 study found that of contemporary models only Claude used em dashes more than professional writers, and ChatGPT used them less.[18]

While still produced today as many AI users don't use the latest AI models, those same latest AI models of late (September 2026 and onward) have more and more often been found to be [hard coded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_code) to not use em dashes at all. This, however, has the unintended side effect of making LLMs not once dare to use any sort of alternate puctuation, so the overall variety has dropped significantly in their output; commas and periods are more likely now than even human text, and now that has become its own sign of AI writing.

### Abrupt cut offs

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Abrupt cut offs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Abrupt_cut_offs)

AI tools used to abruptly stop generating content if an excessive number of tokens had been used for a single response, and further responses required the user to select "continue generating", at least in the case of ChatGPT.

This method is not foolproof, as a malformed copy/paste from one's local computer can also cause this. It may also indicate a [copyright violation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Copyvio) rather than the use of an LLM.

### Outdated *access-date* parameters

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Outdated *access-date* parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Outdated_access-date_parameters)

In some AI-assisted text, citations may include an *access-date* by default, but the date can look unexpectedly old relative to when the edit was made (for example, an article created in December 2025 containing multiple citations with `|access-date=12 December 2024`). However, newer chatbots seldom produce this error, and older *access-date* values can occur legitimately (copied citations, offline work, batch moves/merges).

### Lexical diversity/elegant variation

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing § Lexical diversity/elegant variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Lexical_diversity%2Felegant_variation)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIELEVAR`

*For a non-AI-specific style essay about this, see [Wikipedia:The problem with elegant variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:The_problem_with_elegant_variation).*

*Further information: [Lexical diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_diversity)*

Older AI models may include a repetition penalty, meant to discourage it from reusing words too often.[10] This pattern has also been observed on Wikipedia on a broad level: both when comparing Wikipedia text from before 2023 to Wikipedia text from after 2023, and comparing the older Wikipedia text to "Wikipedia-style articles" generated by GPT-4o-mini and Gemini-1.5-Flash.[15]

Note: If a user adds multiple pieces of AI-generated content in separate edits, this tell may not apply, as each piece of text may have been generated in isolation.

**Examples**

In <mark>the challenging climate of Soviet artistic constraints</mark>, Yankilevsky, alongside other <mark>non-conformist artists</mark>, faced obstacles in expressing <mark>their creativity</mark> freely. Dina Vierny, recognizing <mark>the immense talent</mark> and the struggle <mark>these artists</mark> endured, played a pivotal role in aiding <mark>their artistic aspirations</mark>. [...]
>
> In this new chapter of his life, Yankilevsky found himself amidst a community of <mark>like-minded artists</mark> who, despite diverse styles, shared a common goal—to break free from <mark>the confines of state-imposed artistic norms</mark>, particularly socialist realism. [...]
>
> The move to Paris facilitated an environment where Yankilevsky could further explore and exhibit <mark>his distinctive artistic vision</mark> without <mark>the constraints imposed by the Soviet regime</mark>. Dina Vierny's unwavering support and commitment to the <mark>Russian avant-garde artists</mark> played a crucial role in fostering a space where <mark>their creativity</mark> could flourish, contributing to the rich tapestry of artistic expression in the vibrant cultural landscape of Paris. ### Overuse of boldface

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style § Overuse of boldface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style#Overuse_of_boldface)

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Text formatting § Boldface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Text_formatting#Boldface).*

Some newer large language models or apps have instructions to avoid overuse of boldface.

### Inline-header vertical lists

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style § Inline-header vertical lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style#Inline-header_vertical_lists)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AILIST`

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Lists § Use prose where understood easily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Lists#Use_prose_where_understood_easily).*

AI chatbots output often includes vertical lists formatted in a specific way: an ordered or unordered list where the list marker (number, bullet, dash, etc.) is followed by an inline boldfaced header, separated with a colon from the remaining descriptive text.

Instead of [proper wikitext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H:LIST), a bullet point in an unordered list may appear as a bullet character (•), hyphen (-), en dash (–), [hash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Use_of_Markdown) (#), [emoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing#Emoji_as_formatting), or similar character. Ordered lists (i.e. numbered lists) may use explicit numbers (such as `1.`) instead of standard wikitext. When [copied as bare text appearing on the screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:SCOPY), some of the formatting information is lost, and line breaks may be lost as well.

### Curly quotation marks and apostrophes

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style § Curly quotation marks and apostrophes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Style#Curly_quotation_marks_and_apostrophes)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AICURLY`

*For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see [Wikipedia:Manual of Style § Quotation characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style#Quotation_characters), and [Wikipedia:Manual of Style § Apostrophes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style#Apostrophes).*

ChatGPT and [DeepSeek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeepSeek) typically use curly quotation marks (“...” or ‘...’) instead of straight quotation marks ("..." or '...'). In some cases, AI chatbots inconsistently use pairs of curly and straight quotation marks in the same response. They also tend to use the curly apostrophe (’), the same character as the curly [right single quotation mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_single_quotation_mark), instead of the straight apostrophe ('), such as in [contractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraction_%28grammar%29) and [possessive forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_possessive). They may also do this inconsistently.

Curly quotes alone do not prove LLM use. Directional quotation marks (curly or typographer) are often used in published works written and edited using the [Chicago Manual of Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Manual_of_Style).[4] [Microsoft Word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Word) has a "[smart quotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_quotes)" feature that converts straight quotes to curly quotes. So does the default system-wide configuration on [macOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacOS) and [iOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOS) devices, except on some applications (or if turned off, as may be necessary for [programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming)). Grammar correcting tools such as [LanguageTool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LanguageTool) may also have such a feature. Curly quotation marks and apostrophes are common in professionally typeset works such as major newspapers. Citation tools like [Citer](https://citer.toolforge.org/) may repeat those that appear in the title of a web page: for example,

> McClelland, Mac (2017-09-27). ["When ‘Not Guilty’ Is a Life Sentence"](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/09/27/magazine/when-not-guilty-is-a-life-sentence.html). *The New York Times*. Retrieved 2025-08-03.

Note that Wikipedia allows users to [customize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:CUSTOM) the fonts used to display text. Some fonts display matched curly apostrophes as straight, in which case the distinction is invisible to the user. Additionally, [Gemini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_%28language_model%29) and [Claude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_%28language_model%29) models typically do not use curly quotes.

### Use of Markdown

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup § Use of Markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup#Use_of_Markdown)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:MARKDOWN`, `WP:AIMARKDOWN`

*Further information: [Wikitext wrapped in Markdown code blocks from chatbots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AISIGNS/Wikitext_wrapped_in_Markdown_code_blocks_from_chatbots)*

Wikipedia's prose is formatted in [wikitext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H:WT), a [markup language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markup_language) used to tell Wikipedia's [MediaWiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MediaWiki) software how to format an article. This is a niche markup language, found mostly on Wikipedia, other Wikimedia wikis, and other MediaWiki-based platforms like [Miraheze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miraheze). While LLM-based chatbots do have the ability to produce wikitext, it is neither the most common markup language in their datasets nor the most salient one to their userbase – the overwhelming majority of whom are not Wikipedia editors.

Instead, the default formatting style of most chatbots is Markdown, a markup language conceptually similar to wikitext but much more common. Their system-level instructions often direct them to format article components like lists and headings with Markdown syntax, and the chatbot apps then render that syntax as formatted text on a user's screen. For example, the system prompt for Claude Sonnet 3.5 (November 2024) includes:[1]

> Claude uses Markdown formatting. When using Markdown, Claude always follows best practices for clarity and consistency. It always uses a single space after hash symbols for headers (e.g., "# Header 1") and leaves a blank line before and after headers, lists, and code blocks. For emphasis, Claude uses asterisks or underscores consistently (e.g., italic or bold). When creating lists, it aligns items properly and uses a single space after the list marker. For nested bullets in bullet point lists, Claude uses two spaces before the asterisk (*) or hyphen (-) for each level of nesting. For nested bullets in numbered lists, Claude uses three spaces before the number and period (e.g., "1.") for each level of nesting.

As the above indicates, Markdown syntax is completely different from wikitext. Markdown uses asterisks (*) or underscores (_) instead of single-quotes (') for bold and italic formatting, hash symbols (#) instead of equals signs (=) for section headings, parentheses (()) instead of square brackets ([]) around URLs, and three symbols (---, ***, or ___) instead of four hyphens (----) for thematic breaks.

When told to "generate an article", chatbots often default to using Markdown for the generated output. This formatting is preserved in clipboard text by the copy functions on some chatbot platforms. If instructed to generate content for Wikipedia, the chatbot might "realize" the need to generate Wikipedia-compatible code, and might include a message like Would you like me to ... turn this into actual Wikipedia markup format (`wikitext`)?[a] in its output. If the chatbot is told to proceed, the resulting syntax is often rudimentary, syntactically incorrect, or both. The chatbot might put its attempted wikitext content in a Markdown-style [fenced code block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Wikitext_wrapped_in_Markdown_code_blocks_from_chatbots) (its syntax for [preformatted plain text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:PRE)) surrounded by Markdown-based syntax and content, which may also be preserved by platform-specific copy-to-clipboard functions, leading to a telling footprint of both markup languages' syntax. This might include the appearance of three backticks in the text, such as: `` ```wikitext ``.[b][c]

The presence of faulty wikitext syntax mixed with Markdown syntax is a strong indicator that content is LLM-generated, especially if in the form of a fenced Markdown code block. However, Markdown *alone* is not such a strong indicator. Software developers, researchers, technical writers, and experienced internet users frequently use Markdown in tools like [Obsidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian_%28software%29) and [GitHub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GitHub_Flavored_Markdown), and on platforms like [Reddit](https://support.reddithelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/360043033952-Formatting-Guide), [Discord](https://support.discord.com/hc/en-us/articles/210298617-Markdown-Text-101-Chat-Formatting-Bold-Italic-Underline), and [Slack](https://slack.com/help/articles/202288908-Format-your-messages). Some writing tools and apps, such as [iOS Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOS_Notes), [Google Docs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Docs), and [Windows Notepad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Notepad), support Markdown editing or exporting. The increasing ubiquity of Markdown may also lead new editors to expect or assume Wikipedia to support Markdown by default.

*See also: [Markdown § Examples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markdown#Examples)*

#### Omission of colon-letter strings

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup § Omission of colon-letter strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup#Omission_of_colon-letter_strings)

When copying LLM output containing transclusions of [magic words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:MAGICWORDS) like {{[DEFAULTSORT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template:DEFAULTSORT)}} or links to Wikipedia pages outside of the article mainspace, users sometimes fail to paste the colon and the word right after it.

In some biographical drafts, this manifests as an apparent attempt to transclude Template:DEFAULTSORT, Firstname followed by red links to pages such as Category people where the wikicode should have been typed out as `{{DEFAULTSORT:Surname, Firstname}}` and `[[Category:Living people]]` respectively. - [WP~~**:SHORTCUT**~~](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:SHORTCUT) → WP

#### Entire articles wrapped in `<nowiki></nowiki>` tags

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup § Entire articles wrapped in `<nowiki></nowiki>` tags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup#Entire_articles_wrapped_in_%3Cnowiki%3E%3C%2Fnowiki%3E_tags)

Copying article text from AI tools like ChatGPT behaves differently depending on how ChatGPT presents that text in the UI. Usually, the wikitext will be copied as plaintext, but sometimes ChatGPT will include additional formatting, which VisualEditor will wrap inside `<nowiki></nowiki>` once pasted in.[d] This will cause those `<nowiki></nowiki>` tags to be visible in the article text itself.

While Wikipedia's editing interface does have a button to insert <nowiki> tags, which editors sometimes press by accident, if the whole article is wrapped inside <nowiki>, then the text was almost certainly pasted from somewhere in full. The most likely place for that to be is ChatGPT.

### Non-existent or out-of-place categories

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup § Non-existent or out-of-place categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup#Non-existent_or_out-of-place_categories)

*Wikipedia Shortcuts:* `WP:AIREDCAT`

LLMs may hallucinate non-existent categories, sometimes for generic concepts that *seem like* plausible category titles (or [SEO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine_optimization) keywords), and sometimes because their training set includes obsolete and renamed categories. These will appear as [red links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:REDNOT). You may also find category redirects, such as the longtime spammer favorite [Category:Entrepreneurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Entrepreneurs). More recent LLMs will occasionally [omit punctuation and words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AILINKFAIL) in category markup (e.g. Category people instead of [Category:Living people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Living_people)), visibly breaking them.

Note that the presence of bad or nonexistent categories does not necessarily prove the use of AI by itself, as human editors regularly make similar mistakes. New users are unlikely to be familiar with Wikipedia's categorization system, and older users may be used to now-deprecated categories. Broken categories are, however, valid corroborating evidence if a page also shows other signs of AI editing, and given that it relies on the removal of actual words from a category name rather than just a coding error alone, the "Category people" form of category breakage is a strong AI tell as this is not an error typically made by human editors.

**Examples**

> ```wikitext
> [[Category:American hip hop musicians]]
> ```

— From [this August 2025 revision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:PermanentLink/1304282215) to [Draft:Paytra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Examples/Paytra)

rather than

> ```wikitext
> [[Category:American hip-hop musicians]]
> ```

### Non-existent templates

> **Verified Section Link:** [Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup § Non-existent templates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing/Markup#Non-existent_templates)

LLMs often hallucinate non-existent templates (especially plausible-sounding types of [infoboxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Infoboxes)) and template parameters. These will also appear as red links, and non-existent template parameters in existing templates have no effect. LLMs may also use templates that were deleted after their knowledge cutoff date (such as the [lang-*??* series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Templates_for_discussion/Log/2024_September_27/lang-%3F%3F_templates)).

> | regions = Ganga Valley (from Haryana to Bengal, between the Vindhyas and Himalayas)
> | period = Mesolithic–Early Neolithic (10,000–5,000 BCE)
> | descendants = Gangetic peoples, Indus Valley Civilisation, South Indian populations
> | archaeological_sites = Bhimbetka, Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha, Jhusi, Chirand
> }}
> ```

rather than

> {{Infobox archaeological culture
> | name = Gangetic Hunter-Gatherer (GHG)
> | map = [[File:GHG_reconstruction.png|250px]]
