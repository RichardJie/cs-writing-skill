### 1.1 Patrick H. Winston (MIT OpenCourseWare `RES.TLL-005`) — *How to Speak*

- **Author & Institution**: Prof. Patrick Henry Winston, Director of MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (1972–1997) / MIT CSAIL; MIT OpenCourseWare (`RES.TLL-005`, January IAP 2018).
- **Verified URLs**:
  - Course Portal: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/
  - Lecture Outline & Video Page: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/pages/how-to-speak/
  - Official MIT OCW Full Lecture Transcript PDF (22 pages): https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/bc92763ffa0dad0ecafe44967e834e16_Unzc731iCUY.pdf
- **Cloned Open-Source Skills Based on Winston's Lecture**:
  - `keyuchen21/how-to-speak-skill` (MIT License, SHA `222f743a6e1509011bf892d4384b33395d074b02` — `../upstream-skills/04-defense-grant-and-speaking/keyuchen21-how-to-speak-skill/`): https://github.com/keyuchen21/how-to-speak-skill
  - `Arahmane1007/winston-presentations-skill` (MIT License, SHA `ce96a06adc72518ded5fe17780b38bac802744b3` — `../upstream-skills/04-defense-grant-and-speaking/Arahmane1007-winston-presentations-skill/`): https://github.com/Arahmane1007/winston-presentations-skill

#### Official MIT OCW Lecture Outline (`RES.TLL-005`)
1. `Introduction` (`@00:16`) — Winston's communication formula: *"Your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas, in that order."* Quality is determined by Knowledge ($K$), Practice ($P$), and inherent Talent ($T$), *"and notice that the $T$ is very small. What really matters is what you know."*
2. `Rules of Engagement` (`@03:11`) — Close laptops and phones: *"We humans only have one language processor."* If the audience's language processor is reading email or dense slide text, they stop listening to the speaker.
3. `How to Start` (`@04:15`) —
   - **Do NOT start with a joke**: At the start of a talk, people are still settling in and adjusting to your vocal parameters, so jokes usually fall flat.
   - **Start with an empowerment promise**: *"You want to tell people what they're going to know at the end of the hour that they didn't know at the beginning of the hour. It's an empowerment promise. It's the reason for being here."*
4. `Four Sample Heuristics` (`@05:38`) —
   - **Cycle on the subject**: State the core idea three times across the talk (*"at any given moment, about 20% of you will be fogged out no matter what the lecture is. So if you want to ensure that the probability that everybody gets it is high, you need to say it three times"*).
   - **Build a fence around your idea**: Explicitly distinguish your idea from neighboring ideas so it is not confused with someone else's work (e.g., *"my algorithm might seem similar to Jones's algorithm, except his is exponential, and mine's linear"*).
   - **Verbal punctuation**: Enumerate and provide structural seams (*"The first thing... The second idea... and now, the third idea..."*) so listeners who fogged out have landmark places to *"get back on the bus."*
   - **Ask a question**: Pose a carefully calibrated question (neither so obvious that people are embarrassed to answer, nor so hard that nobody speaks) and **wait up to 7 seconds** of silence for an answer.
5. `The Tools: Time and Place` (`@10:17`) —
   - Best lecture time: **11 AM** (everyone is awake, not post-meal fatigued).
   - **Keep the lights full up**: Dimmed rooms signal humans to go to sleep (*"it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids"*).
   - **Case the room beforehand**: Inspect the venue ahead of time to catch layout/AV weirdnesses; choose a room sized so it is **more than half full**.
6. `The Tools: Boards, Props, and Slides` (`@13:24`) —
   - **Boards & Props for Informing (Teaching)**: Blackboard writing speed matches human idea-absorption speed and gives novice speakers a target for their hands (avoiding the *"hands in pockets"* or *"hands behind back"* crimes). Physical props trigger **empathetic mirroring** (mirror neurons).
   - **Slides for Exposing Ideas (Conference Talks & Job Talks)** — Winston's Slide Rules:
     - *"There are always too many slides, always too many words."*
     - *"You want the slides to be condiments to what you're saying, not the main event."*
     - **Eliminate clutter**: Strip background junk, strip recurring logos, strip redundant slide titles when spoken aloud, strip unnecessary bullet markers, and never read slides verbatim.
     - **Font size**: Approximately **40–50 pt**; *"35 is beginning to get too small, not necessarily because you can't read it, but because you're probably using it to get too many words on the slide."*
     - **Stand close to the slides**: Avoid standing across the stage from the screen, which creates a *"tennis match feeling of shifting back and forth between the slide and the speaker."*
     - **Eliminate the laser pointer crime**: Using a laser pointer forces you to turn your head to the screen, showing the audience the back of your head and breaking eye contact. Instead, **place numbered arrows directly on the slide graphic** and say *"look at that guy at the end of arrow number one."*
     - **Print-and-table "too-heavy" audit**: Print the slides out and lay them on a table to check immediately whether there is enough white space ("air") and imagery versus text walls.
     - ***Hapax legomenon* rule**: A single intentionally overwhelming complexity slide is allowed **at most once per presentation** (*"one per work, one per presentation, one per paper, one per book"*).
7. `Persuading: Oral Exams, Job Talks, Getting Famous` (`@41:30`) —
   - **Oral Exams / Defenses**: The two primary causes of failure are **failure to situate** (placing research in global context) and **failure to practice with non-insiders** (*"your faculty supervisor is not a very good person to help you debug a talk because they, in fact, know what you're doing. And they will, in fact, hallucinate there's material in your presentation that isn't there"* — rehearse with friends who do not know your work).
   - **Job Talks (5-Minute Rule)**: A faculty candidate has **5 minutes** to establish two things: (1) **Vision** (*"a problem that somebody cares about and something new in your approach"*) and (2) **Done something** (enumerating the concrete steps needed to solve the problem and demonstrating results).
   - **Getting Remembered — Winston's Star (The 5 S's)**:
     1. **Symbol** — a visual emblem associated with the work (e.g., Winston's arch).
     2. **Slogan** — a memorable phrase handle on the work (e.g., *"one-shot learning"*).
     3. **Surprise** — a counterintuitive truth (e.g., learning something definite from a single example rather than millions).
     4. **Salient Idea** — a single idea that sticks out, not five competing ideas (e.g., *"near miss"*).
     5. **Story** — how you did it, how it works, and why it matters.
8. `How to Stop: Final Slide, Final Words` (`@53:06` & `@56:35`) —
   - **Put collaborators on the FIRST slide**, never a giant list at the end that dilutes your perceived contribution.
   - **Never end on "Questions?", "Thank You", URL lists, or generic "Conclusions"**: The final slide stays projected for 15–20 minutes during Q&A; squandering that real estate wastes your best opportunity to show who you are. Label the final slide **Contributions** (mirroring your opening steps and stating what you actually delivered).
   - **Do NOT end with "Thank you" as your final spoken words**: Saying *"Thank you for listening"* is a weak move suggesting the audience stayed only out of politeness. Instead, end with a joke (now that listeners are tuned to your voice), a benediction, or **salute the audience** (expressing how stimulating your visit and their questions have been).

### 1.2 Michael Alley (Penn State University) — *The Assertion-Evidence Approach*

- **Author & Institution**: Prof. Michael Alley, Leonhard Center for Enhancement of Engineering Education, Pennsylvania State University (supported by NSF Grant `1323230`; *The Craft of Scientific Presentations*, 2nd ed., Springer, 2013).
- **Verified URLs**:
  - Official Assertion-Evidence Portal: https://www.assertion-evidence.com/
  - Official 5-Step Assertion-Evidence Tutorial: https://www.craftscicom.org/ae_tutorial.html
- **Cloned Open-Source Templates**:
  - `hplgit/MAlley-slide-templates` by Prof. Hans Petter Langtangen (SHA `3c3c91811c3c6cffa1e479588e0c588be1f7e7d6` — `../upstream-skills/04-defense-grant-and-speaking/hplgit-MAlley-slide-templates/`): https://github.com/hplgit/MAlley-slide-templates (LaTeX Beamer `beamer/AE_presentation_template-beamer.tex`, PowerPoint, Keynote, LibreOffice, DocOnce, and HTML5 implementations).

#### Core Principles Extracted from Alley's Official Tutorial
1. **Reject PowerPoint's Defaults (Step 1)**: PowerPoint's default **phrase headline + bulleted list** is the *"biggest single mistake"* in scientific and engineering talks:
   - A topic-phrase headline (e.g., *"Experimental Results"*) fails to communicate the takeaway message.
   - A bulleted body forces listeners to read more text than they can process while listening, crowds graphics, and shrinks visual callouts until they become illegible.
2. **Two Pillars of Assertion-Evidence (Step 2)**:
   - **Build the talk on succinct messages (assertions), not topics**: Write a complete sentence (typically 1–2 lines) at the top of each slide stating the slide's main takeaway claim.
   - **Support assertions with visual evidence, not bullet lists**: Dedicate the slide body to photographs, drawings, diagrams, graphs, data plots, or key equations with readable callouts and generous white space.
3. **Message-First Storyboarding & Speaker Notes (Step 3)**: Before opening slide software, write down the sequence of complete-sentence assertions so they tell a coherent story; move secondary details into the **notes pages** rather than onto the slide canvas.
4. **Extemporaneous Delivery Anchored on Visuals (Step 4)**: Rather than reading bullets off the screen (which serve merely as speaker crutches), fashion spoken sentences on the spot (after planning and practice) to explain the projected visual evidence directly, projecting authority and confidence.

### 1.3 Simon Peyton Jones (Microsoft Research Cambridge) — *How to Give a Great Research Talk*

- **Author & Institution**: Simon Peyton Jones (with John Launchbury & John Hughes, *SIGPLAN Notices* 28(11): 9–12, Nov 1993; Microsoft Research Cambridge updated 43-slide deck).
- **Verified URLs**:
  - Microsoft Research Resource Hub: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/academic-program/give-great-research-talk/
  - Original 1993 Publication Page: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/how-to-give-a-good-research-talk/
  - Official 43-Slide Deck PDF: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/How-to-give-a-great-research-talk.pdf

#### Exact Rules & Structure from Peyton Jones's Official MSR Deck
- **Rhetorical Purpose (Slides 4–5 & 40)**:
  - The purpose of your talk is **NOT** to impress your audience with your brainpower, tell them everything you know, or present all the technical details.
  - The purpose of your talk **IS** to give the audience an **intuitive feel for your idea**, *"make them foam at the mouth with eagerness to read your paper"*, and engage/excite/provoke them.
  - *"Your paper = the beef. Your talk = the beef advertisement. Do not confuse the two."*
- **Know Your Actual Audience (Slides 7–8)**: Your actual audience has never heard of you, wishes they hadn't heard of *cartesian closed endomorphic bifunctors*, and has just had lunch — *"Your mission is to WAKE THEM UP and make them glad they did."*
- **What to Put In — The 20% / 80% Split (Slides 10–15)**:
  1. **Motivation (20%)**: You have **2 minutes** to answer four questions before the audience starts to doze: *Why should I tune into this talk? What is the problem? Why is it an interesting problem? Does this talk describe a worthwhile advance?*
  2. **Your Key Idea (80%)**:
     - Identify **one** specific key idea (*"Say: 'If you remember nothing else, remember this.'"*) and ruthlessly prune everything irrelevant to that goal.
     - **Narrow, deep beats wide, shallow**: Avoid shallow overviews at all costs; cut to the technical "meat" — it is completely fine to cover only part of your paper.
     - **Examples are your main weapon**: Use concrete examples to motivate the work, convey basic intuition, illustrate the idea in action, show extreme cases, and highlight shortcomings. *"When time is short, omit the general case, not the example."*
  3. **There is no 3.**
- **What to Leave Out (Slides 18–23)**:
  - **No outline slide at the start**: *"Conveys near zero information at the start of your talk. Worse, since your audience only gives you 2 minutes before dozing, you've just lost them."* (Optionally show an orientation outline *after* motivation and as signposts at pause points.)
  - **Do not present a Related Work slide**: You must know related work cold for Q&A, acknowledge co-authors on the title slide and precursors inline as you go, and **praise the opposition** (*"X's very interesting work does Y; I have extended it to do Z"*), but never put up a bibliography laundry list.
  - **Omit dense technical details**: *"Even though every line is drenched in your blood and sweat, dense clouds of notation will send your audience to sleep."* Present specific aspects only, refer to the paper for full proofs/typing rules, and keep **backup slides** for Q&A.
- **Delivery, Slides & Timing (Slides 25–38)**:
  - **Enthusiasm** is your single most potent weapon (*"If you do not seem excited by your idea, why should the audience be?"*).
  - Write or polish slides the night before so the talk is fresh in your mind.
  - **Never apologise** (*"I didn't have time to prepare"*, *"I don't have time to tell you about this"*).
  - Beat pre-talk **"jelly effect"** (nerves) by deep breathing during the previous talk and **scripting your first few sentences precisely** (*"=> no brain required"*).
  - Face the audience (laptop in front of you, screen toward you; point at the projection screen sparingly, never at your laptop); speak to someone at the back of the room and identify a **nodder**.
  - Treat questions during the talk as a *"golden golden golden opportunity"* to connect; be ready to truncate material.
  - **Avoid revealing bullet points one by one** unless there is a punchline; use animation *very* sparingly.
  - **Absolutely without fail, finish on time**: Audiences stop listening once time is up; simply truncate and conclude — never ask *"would you like me to go on?"*

### 1.4 Jean-luc Doumont (Principiae) — *Trees, Maps, and Theorems*

- **Author & Institution**: Dr. Jean-luc Doumont (PhD in Applied Physics, Stanford University; Ir, Louvain), Principiae (*Trees, maps, and theorems: Effective communication for rational minds*, 2009).
- **Verified URLs**:
  - Principiae Main Portal: https://www.principiae.be/
  - Principiae Online Handouts & Resources Hub: https://www.principiae.be/X0010.php
  - Official Oral Presentation Program Summary PDF: https://www.principiae.be/pdfs/Principiae-compact-presentations.pdf
  - Official *Effective Research Posters* Booklet PDF (v1.01): https://www.principiae.be/pdfs/ERP-1.01.pdf
  - MIT EECS Comm Lab Citation of Doumont's Signal-to-Noise Principle: https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/research-qualifying-examination-rqe/

#### Verified Core Principles from Doumont's Principiae Publications
1. **Maximize Signal-to-Noise Ratio**: Every slide or poster element must maximize **signal** (the intended message and supporting data) while eliminating **noise** (decorative templates, repeated clutter, unneeded ink, redundant gridlines, or extraneous equations/text).
2. **State Messages as Full Conjugated Sentences ("So What", Not "What")**: State each message verbally as a complete sentence with a **subject and conjugated verb** (left-aligned, maximum two lines, broken at a meaningful syntactic boundary), expressing the *so what* (conclusion/takeaway) rather than a bare topic label.
3. **Attention-Guided Layout & Constraints**: Use layout to reveal logical structure rather than decoration; achieve harmony through strict constraints — **a single typeface in just a few sizes**, **a limited set of meaningful colors** (often one accent color besides black), **a few alignment axes**, and generous empty space (*"distribute space, but resist the temptation to fill it"*). Do not underline words or set text in ALL CAPS.
4. **Do Not Let Corporate/Institutional Templates Ruin Clarity**: Templates are built for branding, often at the expense of readability and visual clarity; prioritize an effective, high-signal layout while keeping institutional font/colors/logo tasteful and unobtrusive.

### 1.5 Philip E. Bourne (*PLOS Computational Biology* 2007) — *Ten Simple Rules for Making Good Oral Presentations*

- **Author & Citation**: Philip E. Bourne (UC San Diego), *PLoS Comput Biol* 3(4): e77 (2007).
- **Verified URLs**: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1857815/ | DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.0030077

#### Verbatim Ten Rules & Operational Benchmarks
1. **Rule 1: Talk to the Audience** — Tailor scope to the audience's background, knowledge level, and what they hope to get out of the session, rather than delivering an off-topic canned talk.
2. **Rule 2: Less is More** — Inexperienced speakers try to prove how much they know by cramming too much material, losing the main message, talking too fast, and curtailing Q&A. Express mastery through a concise, provocative talk that sparks active Q&A dialog.
3. **Rule 3: Only Talk When You Have Something to Say** — Do not abuse the audience's time with uninteresting, premature preliminary material.
4. **Rule 4: Make the Take-Home Message Persistent** — **1-Week 3-Point Benchmark**: One week after your presentation, an audience member should be able to recall **three key points** — and they must be the exact three points you intended.
5. **Rule 5: Be Logical** — Structure the talk as a story with a clear stage-setting beginning, logical middle, and big finish where the take-home message lands unmistakably.
6. **Rule 6: Treat the Floor as a Stage** — Be engaging within your natural limits (do not force jokes or anecdotes if they are unnatural to you).
7. **Rule 7: Practice and Time Your Presentation** — Stick to what you rehearsed; never improvise onto tangents you know less about than the audience. Never deliver an important talk for the first time to external peers — rehearse in lab group meetings first.
8. **Rule 8: Use Visuals Sparingly but Effectively** — **1-Visual-Per-Minute Cap**: *"If you have more than one visual for each minute you are talking, you have too many and you will run over time."* Do not overload slides or read them verbatim.
9. **Rule 9: Review Audio and/or Video of Your Presentations** — Watch/listen to recordings of your own talks to spot and break nervous habits and pacing violations.
10. **Rule 10: Provide Appropriate Acknowledgments** — Credit actual contributors and funding clearly (often at the beginning or inline at the point of their contribution) rather than rushing gratuitous lists after running out of time.

### 1.6 Michael D. Ernst (UW CSE) & Matt Might — Technical & Academic Talk Principles

- **Michael D. Ernst (University of Washington CSE)** — *How to give a technical presentation (how to give a scientific talk)* (2005; updated March 21, 2022)
  - **Verified URL**: https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html
  - **Extracted Rules**:
    - **Three-Part Persuasion Core**: Convince the audience that (1) the problem is **worthwhile** (real and useful), (2) the problem is **hard** (not already solved trivially), and (3) **you have solved it**.
    - **Whiteboard Pre-Slide Test**: Explain your ideas verbally to a non-expert before opening slide software; organize slides around the order that worked verbally (~1 slide per minute).
    - **Never Paste Raw Paper Tables**: Reformat tables specifically for slides, pruning non-essential rows/columns.
    - **Slide Titles & Bookends**: Every slide needs a unique descriptive title stating its specific contribution. Slide 2 (right after Title) must be concrete **Motivation/Example**, **never** an Outline slide (save section-highlighted outlines for talks $>30$ min). End on a **Contributions/Conclusions** slide and **leave it up during Q&A** (never end on Future Work, *"Questions?"*, *"Thank you"*, or *"The End"*).
    - **Pixel-for-Pixel Zero-Jitter Builds**: When building a slide incrementally across frames, all shared elements must remain in the **exact same pixel position** (verify by flipping rapidly back and forth); highlight newly added elements.
    - **Telegraphic Density & Visual Hygiene**: *"3 lines of text for a bullet point is always too much, and 2 full lines is usually too much."* Never read diagrams component-by-component; never use generic stock clip art (e.g., a padlock for security or a frustrated person at a PC); match diagram background to slide background (prefer dark text on white/light background).

- **Matt Might (University of Utah / UAB)** — *10 tips on how to give an academic talk*
  - **Verified URL**: https://matt.might.net/articles/academic-presentation-tips/
  - **Verbatim 10 Tips**:
    1. *The audience determines the talk* (never pitch a room of hundreds to just 1–2 narrow specialists).
    2. *Practice almost makes perfect* (rehearse ~10 times without memorizing a rigid script).
    3. *Nervous energy is exploitable.*
    4. *Every talk should motivate a problem.*
    5. *An academic talk is about an idea, not a paper.*
    6. *Slides must not overwhelm the viewer.*
    7. *Images and diagrams are better than text.*
    8. *Math's benefit must outweigh the loss of attention.*
    9. *Style matters.*
    10. *Questions are not random* (recurring questions signal structural gaps to fix or turn into backup slides).

### 2.1 Classic CS Conference Oral Blueprint — Mark D. Hill (UW-Madison) & David A. Patterson

- **Authors & Institutions**: Prof. Mark D. Hill (Computer Sciences Dept., University of Wisconsin-Madison, *Oral Presentation Advice*, 1992/1997), incorporating Prof. David A. Patterson (UC Berkeley, *How to Give a Bad Talk*, circa 1983).
- **Verified URL**: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/conference-talk.html

#### Mark D. Hill's *Generic Conference Talk Outline* (~12 Slides for a 20-Minute Talk, ~2 Min/Slide)
- **Rhetorical Goals**: (1) Leave listeners with a clear picture of the gist of your contribution, and (2) whet their appetite to read your paper (not replace it). Address layered audiences by pitching the **Forecast** and **Summary** to everyone and the **Body** to specialists.
- **Slide-by-Slide Allocation**:
  1. **Title / Author / Affiliation** (`1 slide`)
  2. **Forecast** (`1 slide`) — The "oral abstract": gist of the problem attacked and the single insight found.
  3. **Outline** (`1 slide`, or placed at the bottom of the title slide for predictability).
  4. **Motivation and Problem Statement** (`1–2 slides`) — Why anyone should care (researchers chronically overestimate how much listeners know about their problem).
  5. **Related Work** (`0–1 slides`) — Cover superficially or omit; refer to the paper.
  6. **Methods** (`1 slide`) — Cover quickly in short talks; refer to the paper.
  7. **Results** (`4–6 slides`) — Main body of the talk: cover **key** results deeply rather than all results superficially; interpret numbers to yield insights; **do not put up large tables of numbers**.
  8. **Summary** (`1 slide`) — Tell them what you told them.
  9. **Future Work** (`0–1 slides`, optional).
  10. **Backup Slides** (`0–3 slides`, uncounted in talk total) — Ready for expected Q&A on glossed-over details, method/result shortcomings, and future work.

#### David A. Patterson's *Ten Commandments of How to Give a Bad Talk* (Annotated by Mark D. Hill)
1. *Thou shalt not be neat* (ignoring spelling, grammar, legibility).
2. *Thou shalt not waste space* (cramming slides to "save transparencies").
3. *Thou shalt not covet brevity* (writing full paragraphs and reading every word).
4. *Thou shalt cover thy naked slides* (artificial suspense).
5. *Thou shalt not write large* (using tiny fonts illegible from the back).
6. *Thou shalt not use color* (flat, unemphasized walls of monochrome text).
7. *Thou shalt not illustrate* (refusing pictures/diagrams).
8. *Thou shalt not make eye contact* (averting eyes and blocking the screen).
9. *Thou shalt not skip slides in a long talk* (talking faster and skipping conclusions when out of time).
10. *Thou shalt not practice* (*"Commandment X is most important. Even if you break the other nine, this one can save you."*).

### 2.3 Spotlight, Lightning & Rapid-Fire Talks — Christopher J. Lortie (*PLOS Computational Biology* 2017)

- **Author & Citation**: Christopher J. Lortie (York University & UCSB NCEAS), *Ten simple rules for short and swift presentations*, *PLoS Comput Biol* 13(3): e1005373 (2017).
- **Verified URLs**: https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005373 | DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005373

#### Verbatim Ten Rules for Spotlight / Lightning / Auto-Advancing Talks (e.g., 3–6 Min / PechaKucha)
- **Core Warning**: Simply shrinking or rushing a 15–20 minute talk fails; reduce Bourne's 3 take-home messages down to **one singular primary message**.
1. **Rule 1: Plan a clear story** — Eliminate detours, tangents, and side anecdotes; 5–6 minutes or 20 rapid slides leave zero room for a story within a story.
2. **Rule 2: Provide only one major point per slide** — Every slide must represent one step forward in the narrative arc.
3. **Rule 3: Limit use of text** — Treat slides like scientific figures; consider a **2-slide show-and-tell pair**: maximize a figure full-bleed on Slide $N$, then put a single brief sentence stating the finding/implication on Slide $N+1$.
4. **Rule 4: Use simple visuals** — Never cut-and-paste dense paper figures that require viewers to search or mentally rotate labels. Limit slides requiring non-cursory visual parsing to **at most one** key quantitative figure, paired with a planned vocal pause so the audience can absorb it.
5. **Rule 5: Develop a consistent theme** — Keep style, graphical design, language, and imagery uniform so cognitive scanning goes entirely to what changes; avoid decorative canned templates.
6. **Rule 6: Repeat critical messages twice using different visuals** — Because 15–20 seconds per slide is fleeting, restate the core finding once using a second visual or restatement slide (*"Three is a crowd... Stick with only one repetition"* — never duplicate the exact same slide twice).
7. **Rule 7: Use the principle of parsimony in explanations** — Compress statistics, sampling, experimental design, and implementation into **at most two succinct sentences within a 15–20 second interval**.
8. **Rule 8: Allocate more than one slide to effectively end the narrative** — Begin closing the singular story arc before the final slide (e.g., slides 16–17 of a 20-slide rapid deck) rather than terminating abruptly.
9. **Rule 9: Use the final slide for contact information and links to additional resources** — Because rapid-fire/spotlight sessions rarely allow live Q&A, display email, website, acknowledgments, and a **published URL to the slide deck itself**.
10. **Rule 10: Use timed practice** — Rehearse standing up, out loud, without notes, with auto-advance enabled, and **practice with a few fewer seconds per slide than allotted** to buffer projector lag and stage transitions.

### 2.4 Scientific Conference Posters — Erren & Bourne, Doumont, Purrington, Alley, MIT EECS Comm Lab & `#betterposter`

#### A. Thomas C. Erren & Philip E. Bourne (*PLOS Computational Biology* 2007) — *Ten Simple Rules for a Good Poster Presentation*
- **Citation & Verified URLs**: *PLoS Comput Biol* 3(5): e102 (2007) | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1876493/ | DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.0030102
- **Verbatim Ten Rules**:
  1. **Rule 1: Define the Purpose** (spark discussion, collaboration, or standalone summary when absent).
  2. **Rule 2: Sell Your Work in Ten Seconds** (pose a decisive question in an "elevator" impression).
  3. **Rule 3: The Title Is Important** (short, sharp, newspaper-headline clarity comprehensible to broad attendees).
  4. **Rule 4: Poster Acceptance Means Nothing** (endorsement comes only from rigorous science presented clearly on the board).
  5. **Rule 5: Many of the Rules for Writing a Good Paper Apply to Posters, Too** (succinct motivation, hypothesis, major results, conclusions calibrated to non-specialists).
  6. **Rule 6: Good Posters Have Unique Features Not Pertinent to Papers** (viewed from a distance, interactive with your presence, allows handouts and broader discussion).
  7. **Rule 7: Layout and Format Are Critical** — Leave generous white space (Keith Richards' *"bare canvas"* principle); guide wandering eyes across frames with explicit arrows or numbering; **never use smaller than `24 pt` font**, and place main points at **eye level**.
  8. **Rule 8: Content Is Important, but Keep It Concise** — Replace word masses with graphics (*"clear portrayal of complexity"*); make **Conclusions** immediately visible (passersby scan Title $\rightarrow$ Conclusions $\rightarrow$ Results $\rightarrow$ Methods).
  9. **Rule 9: Posters Should Have Your Personality** (include a presenter photo so attendees can recognize you later at the venue).
  10. **Rule 10: The Impact of a Poster Happens Both During and After the Poster Session** (let visitors read without badgering them, engage multiple visitors via eye contact, provide A4 handouts / online poster URL / contact info).

#### B. Jean-luc Doumont (Principiae Booklet v1.01, 2017) — *Effective Research Posters*
- **Verified URL**: https://www.principiae.be/pdfs/ERP-1.01.pdf
- **Extracted Design & Delivery Rules**:
  - **1-Meter & A0-to-A4 Sanity Check**: Every text item must be readable from **`0.5 to 1.5 m` away**; equivalently, **an `A0` poster printed on `A4` paper must remain completely readable** (which also produces the ideal souvenir handout).
  - **Clarification vs. Deepening Questions Metric**: *Clarification questions* (*"What's the vertical axis?"*) diagnose a weak poster; *deepening questions* (*"That's interesting! How did you measure it?"*) confirm standalone visual clarity.
  - **Two Anti-Patterns**: (1) **Do not design a poster like a chronological paper** (Abstract $\rightarrow$ Intro $\rightarrow$ Methods $\rightarrow$ Results $\rightarrow$ Discussion $\rightarrow$ References); (2) **Do not format a poster like packing a suitcase** (squeezing items into leftover gaps).
  - **Message Architecture**: Left-aligned 2-line main message title (*what*) + first names/photo (*who*); minimal context + research gap (*need*) + first-person past-tense research verb (*what you did*) + **upfront conclusion**; every modular box headed by a full conjugated sentence and high signal-to-noise graphic.
  - **"Using the Back of the Poster" & 2-Minute Pitch**: Print `A4` handouts with the poster on the front and extra methods, readable references, and contact info (or a QR code) on the back; prepare *"pick-up lines"* for passersby and a **2-minute core story** expandable to **5 minutes** on interest.

#### C. Colin Purrington — *Designing Conference Posters*
- **Verified URL**: https://colinpurrington.com/tips/poster-design/
- **Extracted Section Word-Count & Typography Rules**:
  - **Hard Word Budget**: Keep total poster text to **`800–1,200` words (`<= 1,000` words target**: e.g., ~`700` words body + ~`300` words figure/table legends) so a visitor can read the entire poster in **5–10 minutes**:
    - **Title**: 1–2 lines, **sentence case** (never ALL CAPS; avoid colons if they spill to 3 lines).
    - **Abstract**: **Omit** (*"a poster is an abstract of your research"*), unless strictly mandated by the meeting.
    - **Introduction**: ~`200` words (written for an intelligent non-specialist; minimal acronyms; include an illustrative visual).
    - **Materials and Methods**: ~`200` words (flowcharts/labeled setup photos over prose).
    - **Results**: ~`200` words + standalone figure legends (largest visual arena; prefer figures over tables).
    - **Conclusions**: ~`200` words (restate major result, hypothesis verdict, real-world relevance, and future step).
    - **Acknowledgments** (~`40` words) + **Further Information** (~`20` words: email/URL/QR).
  - **Typography & Graph Hygiene**: Prefer horizontal layout over portrait (portrait forces stooping); keep text box line width to **`45–65` characters** with uniform column widths; use colorblind-safe palettes (avoid red/green; check via simulator); **delete default chart legend keys and label series directly**; align Y-axis labels horizontally in sentence case; strip colored graph backgrounds, gridlines, boxes, and 3D chart effects.

#### D. Michael Alley (Penn State) & MIT EECS Communication Lab — Poster Pitch & `#betterposter`
- **Michael Alley, *The Craft of Scientific Posters*** (https://www.craftscicom.org/posters.html): Focus on 1–2 most important experiments rather than cramming four; **avoid paragraphs longer than 4 sentences**; link overflow work via QR code; documents the evolution from `#betterposter` (1/3 central single-sentence takeaway + QR code) to MIT's `#evenbetterposters`.
- **MIT EECS Communication Lab, *Poster* CommKit** (https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/poster/): Defines the **5-Part 20-Second Poster Pitch** for answering *"So, what's all this about?"*:
  1. *Something that every single person in the room cares about*
  2. *Why we need to know/do more about that thing (gap)*
  3. *What you did in this project*
  4. *What your results mean*
  5. *How your results contribute to the thing everyone cares about*
- **Mike Morrison `#betterposter` & Open-Source LaTeX Class**:
  - Upstream OSF Repository: https://osf.io/ef53g/
  - Cloned LaTeX Class: `rafaelbailo/betterposter-latex-template` (GPL-3.0, SHA `30baa8ea30da2a75fbcd69b3772071ea87e52648` — `../upstream-skills/02-paper-to-slides-and-posters/rafaelbailo-betterposter-latex-template/`, https://github.com/rafaelbailo/betterposter-latex-template), providing `\betterposter` three-zone layout (left silent-presenter column, central giant plain-English finding + `\qrcode`, right technical "ammo bar" for 1-on-1 deep dives).

## Part III — Thesis Proposals, Qualifying Exams & Master/PhD Defenses (开题报告、资格考核、中期检查与硕博毕业答辩)

### 3.1 International Thesis Proposal, Qualifying Exam & Dissertation Defense Guides

#### A. MIT EECS Communication Lab — *Thesis Proposal*
- **Author & Verified URL**: Helen Xu, MIT EECS Communication Lab — https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/thesis-proposal/
- **Extracted Structure & Principles**:
  - **Dual Committee Role & Contract**: A thesis proposal acts as a **contract** between candidate and committee (who act as both *coaches* checking feasibility and *gatekeepers* checking novelty/mastery), protecting the student against unbounded scope creep.
  - **25% / 75% Balance Rule**: Spend roughly **25% on background/related work** and **75% on what you propose to do, preliminary results, and a completion timeline**.
  - **Heilmeier Brainstorming Gate**: Structure proposal motivation and plan by answering the 6 core Heilmeier questions (problem, current limits, novelty & why it will succeed, impact, time/cost/risk, periodic success metrics).
  - **Granular Timeline & Credibility**: Provide a timeline in **multi-month milestones** (granular enough to audit feasibility, not so granular that minor hiccups break it) backed by concrete preliminary experiments, calculations, or simulations rather than vague promises to *"investigate."*

#### B. MIT EECS Communication Lab — *Research Qualifying Examination (RQE)*
- **Author & Verified URL**: Mazdak Abulnaga, MIT EECS Communication Lab — https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/research-qualifying-examination-rqe/
- **Extracted Oral Exam Protocol**:
  - **30 + 30 Min Interactive Format**: For a 1-hour exam before faculty examiners (excluding the advisor), plan a **30-minute presentation + 30 minutes of questions interspersed throughout** (*"Don't expect to get through even your first slide without being interrupted by a question!"*).
  - **Audience Tailoring & Slide Signal-to-Noise**: Tailor domain background to committee members' adjacent specialties (e.g., explain clinical context to ML theorists); use descriptive claim titles instead of *"Methods"*; place secondary equations/definitions in smaller text at the slide bottom or in **backup slides**.
  - **MIT EECS Q&A Response Templates** (verbatim structure from MIT CommKit table):
    - *How is your method different from A?* $\rightarrow$ *"Our method offers advantages in terms of X, Y, and Z."*
    - *Why didn't you compare with `<unfamiliar method>`?* $\rightarrow$ *"Unfortunately, I am not familiar with that exact method, but we did compare with X. Let me tell you about it..."*
    - *Why does your graph dip at this point?* $\rightarrow$ *"We hypothesize that this is because of effect X."*
    - *How long does training take / how to improve?* $\rightarrow$ *"It takes T amount of time, which is fairly slow. We plan to improve by using optimization technique X."*

#### C. MIT CEE Communication Lab — *Handling the Q&A Session*
- **Author & Verified URL**: Matthew Goss, MIT CEE Communication Lab — https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/cee/commkit/handling-the-question-and-answer-session/
- **Three Committee Question Categories & "I Don't Know" Boundary**:
  1. **Clarify** — Resolving skipped steps or jargon mismatches across committee members from outside your sub-area.
  2. **Justify / Explain** — Probing underlying method assumptions, error sources, and limitations (respond non-defensively with figure evidence or acknowledge valid extensions).
  3. **Extrapolate / Reach** — Pushing beyond the thesis scope. Per the MIT CEE Doctoral Program Guide, *"committee members should push questions to the point at which the student says, 'I don't know.'"* Never fabricate answers; state honestly what you do not know and outline **how you would test or derive it** given time/resources.

#### D. Michael Alley & Prof. Karen A. Thole (Penn State) — *Tutorial for Creating a PhD or Master's Defense Presentation*
- **Authors & Verified URL**: Prof. Michael Alley & Distinguished Prof. Karen A. Thole (Dept. Head of Mechanical Engineering, Penn State; adviser of 70+ MS/PhD theses, with Best Dissertation Awardee Dr. Jacob Snyder) — https://www.assertion-evidence.com/dissertation_defense.html
- **The "Focused Slice + Scope Map" Defense Principle**:
  - Even when a PhD comprises 5+ published papers, **do not try to present every paper in the defense**; rushing through everything destroys comprehension.
  - Instead, use a **field-wide scope diagram** (e.g., Snyder's fishbone diagram highlighting in yellow the specific subset of variables tackled in the talk) to show the breadth of the full dissertation upfront, explicitly state that today's talk covers a focused slice in understandable depth, and let the audience simultaneously grasp both the deep technical rigor and the scale of the overall PhD.

#### E. Valdosta State University & UIUC Graduate College — Dissertation Defense Checklists
- **Valdosta State University, *Preparing a Presentation for Your Dissertation Defense: Guidelines*** (https://www.valdosta.edu/colleges/education/deans-office/documents/dissertation-defense-guide.pdf):
  - Cap presentation at **$\le 30$ minutes**; adapt proposal slides by **summarizing Chapters 1–3** (Background, Literature/Framework, Methodology) and **focusing the bulk of slides on Chapters 4–5** (Findings, Conclusions, Implications, Limitations).
  - Keep Researcher's Background *very brief*; **number all Research Questions** so committee members can reference them directly during Q&A; keep **References to 1 slide** listing only works cited on the slides.
- **UIUC GradLIFE (Nitasha Mathayas), *How to Defend Your Dissertation, Virtually*** (https://blogs.illinois.edu/view/6397/808052):
  - Replace physical pointing / laggy virtual laser pointers with **built-in bolding and subtle slide highlights**; use a **dual-screen setup** (slides on primary screen, Zoom video/chat on secondary screen — or reserve empty slide margin if single-screen so attendee video tiles do not cover slide text); pre-share final slide PDF link with committee hours beforehand; separate recorded public room from unrecorded committee deliberation breakout room.

### 3.3 Chinese Graduate Advisors' Guides on ScienceNet (科学网实名学者开题与答辩实战指南)

1. **刘进平教授 (Prof. Liu Jinping, 海南大学 / Hainan University, 科学网实名博文)** — *《研究生开题报告大忌》*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://blog.sciencenet.cn/blog-39731-1554338.html
   - **Five Fatal Pitfalls in Thesis Proposal Defenses (开题五大忌)**:
     1. **Unprofessional PPT Craft (PPT 极不规范)**: Missing a clear technical route diagram (缺技术路线), omitting grant/project acknowledgment when applicable, omitting key references, **copying figures without citing sources (引图不注出处)**, crowding slides with tiny wall-to-wall text and zero figures, and reading verbatim off the screen without facing the panel.
     2. **Imbalanced Time Allocation (时间分配失衡)**: Spending **10 minutes out of a 15-minute slot** on generic introduction/background without arriving at the core research question, or under-allocating time to research contents and technical route while over-talking preliminary trivia.
     3. **Dismissive Attitude Toward Proposal Review (开题心态不正)**: Reusing raw group-meeting slides, throwing slides together the night before, or treating committee diagnostic questions as hostile nitpicking rather than expert guidance.
     4. **Superficial Literature Mastery (文献调研不够)**: Answering *"不知道、不清楚"* ("I don't know / unclear") to basic state-of-the-art questions in one's own subfield.
     5. **Miscalibrated Doctoral Scope (博士课题份量不准)**: Substituting sheer repetitive workload for theoretical depth (只有工作量无理论深度，试图用工作量弥补创新不足), proposing an overly broad/scattered topic that cannot concentrate firepower, or relying on outdated single-method routines.

2. **张忆文教授 (Prof. Zhang Yiwen, 华侨大学计算机科学与技术学院 / Huaqiao University CS, 科学网实名博文)** — *《研究生开题容易忽视的几个问题》*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://blog.sciencenet.cn/blog-827932-1464358.html
   - **Four Overlooked Proposal Failures in CS & Engineering**:
     1. **Misunderstanding the Goal of Opening Reports (未搞清开题目的)**: The sole purpose of an opening report is to convince the committee of two things — **"the problem is worth doing" (有意义)** and **"you can actually pull it off" (能做到)**. Spending **4 minutes of a 10-minute talk** on textbook background leaves the talk top-heavy (头重脚轻).
     2. **Confusing an Opening Proposal with a Finished Thesis (把开题报告当成毕业论文写)**: Stuffing the "planned research contents" with low-level completed equations/steps while leaving the "preliminary work & feasibility basis" nearly empty — the reverse of what committees check.
     3. **Oversized Titles or Disjointed Patchwork (题目过大或内容拼凑)**: E.g., a Master's student titling a thesis *"Research on XX of Large Models"* that is impossible to finish within one year of effective research time, or stitching together 3 unrelated sub-experiments with no unifying thread (which later fails external blind review).
     4. **Literature Laundry Lists (文献综述随意堆砌)**: Listing *"Author A did X; Author B did Y"* chronologically without synthesizing how far the field has progressed, what exact bottleneck remains, and how the proposed work differs from existing papers.

3. **马臻教授 (Prof. Ma Zhen, 复旦大学环境科学与工程系 / Fudan University, 刊发于《中国研究生》2024年第3期 / 科学网精选)** — *《学位论文答辩：唱好“独角戏”》*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://blog.sciencenet.cn/blog-71964-1428953.html
   - **Six Actionable Rules for Master's & PhD Defenses**:
     1. **Prevent Structural Imbalance (避免各板块“失衡”)**: Neither jump straight into raw data tables while omitting research background and prior scholars' groundwork, nor drag out the introduction so long that your own thesis contributions are rushed at the end.
     2. **Calibrate "Conclusions & Outlook" — Never Self-Sabotage (切忌在展望中过多自我贬低)**: Do not omit the outlook slide, **but never list a string of self-incriminating flaws** (*"缺了这个、缺了那个"*), which invites committee pushback (*"既然缺了这么多，为什么不研究好了再来答辩？"*). Reframe boundaries constructively in forward-looking language (e.g., *"这些催化剂的开发，为后续应用研究和机理研究提供了新的契机"*).
     3. **Mandatory Slide Page Numbers (PPT 右下角必须有页码)**: Keep slides clean and scannable, and **always place visible page numbers in the bottom-right corner of every slide** so committee members can jot down exact slide numbers and jump directly to a questioned chart during Q&A.
     4. **Never Distribute Effort Equally Across Chapters (多章节切忌“平均施力”)**: Spend prime time on the core methodological/mechanistic breakthrough chapter; for later chapters that apply a similar paradigm to an extended system (*“依样画葫芦/换体系”*), summarize the design logic and headline findings concisely.
     5. **Rehearse the Opening 1–2 Minutes Cold & Stabilize Hands**: Memorize the opening 1–2 minutes smoothly to conquer stage fright, avoid pacing nervously or shaking the laser pointer wildly on screen, and finalize/print draft resolution logistics with the advisor ahead of time.
     6. **Q&A Composure — Never Snap Back at Examiners (面对质疑严禁任性顶撞)**: Never retort defensively (*"不可以吗？"* or *"我都发了 EST 了，难道还不能毕业吗？"*). Acknowledge constructive critiques respectfully, write down suggestions on paper on the spot, and *"give examiners a graceful off-ramp"* (给评委台阶下).

4. **唐常杰教授 (Prof. Tang Changjie, 四川大学计算机学院 / Sichuan University CS, 刊发于《中国科学报》2012-05-22)** — *《轻松自信去答辩》*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://news.sciencenet.cn/sbhtmlnews/2012/5/258318.shtm
   - **"Six Things a Thesis Defense Is NOT" (毕业论文答辩不是什么)**:
     1. **Not a PPT-reading contest (不是读 PPT 的朗读比赛)** — never turn your back to read dense text walls;
     2. **Not a general survey lecture (不是科技形势报告)** — stories about *"what predecessors did and didn't do"* **should NOT exceed `10%` of total talk time**;
     3. **Not a diplomatic press briefing (不是外交部发言人答记者问)** — avoid evasive bureaucratic non-answers;
     4. **Not a cutthroat debate tournament (不是针锋相对、一剑封喉的辩论赛)**;
     5. **Not a promotional infomercial aimed at examiners**;
     6. **Not a rubber-stamp formality (更不是走过场)**.
   - **Q&A Defense Triad**: **一慎 (Prudence)**, **二诚 (Honesty — never fabricate answers when caught on an unmeasured edge case)**, **三谦虚 (Humility)**.
   - **Four Journalism Principles for Defense Storytelling (向新闻报道学四法)**:
     1. **开门见山 (Lead with the headline)**: Explicitly state Innovation Points 1, 2, and 3 right up front after brief motivation;
     2. **新鲜性与真实性 (Novelty & Verifiability)**;
     3. **夹叙夹议 (Interleave results with insight/interpretation)** rather than dumping raw numbers without meaning;
     4. **首尾呼应 (Bookend symmetry)**: Close by echoing Contributions 1, 2, and 3 cleanly.

### 4.1 DARPA Official *Heilmeier Catechism* & Open-Source Heilmeier Skill

- **Author & Institution**: George H. Heilmeier (Director of DARPA, 1975–1977), Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA).
- **Verified Official URL**: https://www.darpa.mil/about/heilmeier-catechism
- **Cloned Open-Source Skill**: `RealZYZhang/paper-reader-heilmeier` (MIT License, SHA `98568472d35598be422fa70b0da698f33773e0d1`, archived in `../upstream-skills/04-defense-grant-and-speaking/RealZYZhang-paper-reader-heilmeier/`): https://github.com/RealZYZhang/paper-reader-heilmeier

#### The Eight Verbatim Questions of DARPA's *Heilmeier Catechism*
DARPA program managers and proposers use these exact eight questions to evaluate every high-risk, high-payoff research program pitch:
1. **"What are you trying to do? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon."**
2. **"How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?"**
3. **"What is new in your approach and why do you think it will be successful?"**
4. **"Who cares? If you are successful, what difference will it make?"**
5. **"What are the risks?"**
6. **"How much will it cost?"**
7. **"How long will it take?"**
8. **"What are the mid-term and final 'exams' to check for success?"**

*(In `RealZYZhang/paper-reader-heilmeier/SKILL.md`, Questions 1–8 are adapted into a 7-section technical critique spine where Questions 1 and 3 strictly forbid reviewer opinion and require zero-jargon opening framing + exact mathematical/mechanistic differentiation, while Questions 4, 5, and 6 explicitly separate paper claims from first-person critical risk/cost evaluation.)*

### 4.2 European Research Council (ERC) Starting & Consolidator Grant Interview Guides

1. **KTH Royal Institute of Technology Research Office** — *ERC Starting and Consolidator Grant Interview Guide*:
   - **Verified Official PDF URL**: https://intra.kth.se/polopoly_fs/1.722387.1600689954!/ERC%20Interview%20Guide%20170426%20RO.pdf
   - **Format & Panel Composition**: Strict **5–12 minute presentation** (exact minute and slide limits set per ERC panel — some panels restrict candidates to **as few as 1 slide** or even zero slides) followed immediately by **15–20 minutes of intensive Q&A** before **15–20 panel members** who are broad disciplinary generalists rather than narrow niche specialists (at least **3 "lead members"** have read the full proposal and external specialist reviews in detail).
   - **Core Grant Pitch Architecture**:
     - Do **not** waste time reciting your CV (the panel already has it); include **1 dedicated slide on why YOU and your team structure can lead this ambitious program**, plus brief **updates since submission** (newly accepted papers, preliminary breakthroughs).

   - **Nine Verbatim Sample Panel Questions Published by KTH**:
     - *"What is the single most important contribution you expect from this project?"*

     - *"Why do you need an ERC grant of this size (and this specific team composition) rather than a smaller national grant?"*
     - *"Reviewer 3 raised a concern about [specific methodological bottleneck] — how do you address this?"*
     - *"Who are your main international competitors, and what gives you a decisive edge over them?"*
     - *"How do the different work packages depend on each other?"*
     - *"What will you personally do in the lab/project versus your PhD students and postdocs?"*
     - *"Where do you see this research field in 5 to 10 years if your hypothesis holds?"*

### 4.3 Philip E. Bourne & Leo M. Chalupa (*PLOS Computational Biology* 2006) — *Ten Simple Rules for Getting Grants*

- **Authors & Venue**: Philip E. Bourne (UC San Diego) & Leo M. Chalupa (UC Davis), *PLOS Computational Biology*, Vol. 2, Issue 2, e12 (2006), DOI `10.1371/journal.pcbi.0020012`.
- **Verified URL**: https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.0020012
- **Verbatim Ten Rules**:
  1. **Rule 1: Be Novel, But Not Too Novel** — Ground bold ideas in credible preliminary evidence so reviewers neither dismiss the project as incremental nor reject it as speculative fantasy.
  2. **Rule 2: Include the Appropriate Background and Preliminary Data as Required** — Marshal just enough literature context and pilot proof-of-concept to establish feasibility.
  3. **Rule 3: Find the Appropriate Funding Mechanism, Read the Associated Request for Applications Very Carefully, and Respond Specifically to That Request**.
  4. **Rule 4: Follow the Guidelines for Submission Very Carefully and Comply Explicitly**.
  5. **Rule 5: Obey the Three C's — Concise, Clear, and Complete**.
  6. **Rule 6: Remember, Reviewers Are People, Too** — Design clear visual schematics, readable typography, and upfront executive framing for fatigued panelists reviewing dozens of proposals under tight deadlines.
  7. **Rule 7: Timing and Internal Review Are Important** — Subject proposals and pitches to harsh pre-review by colleagues outside your immediate niche well ahead of deadlines.
  8. **Rule 8: Know Your Program Officer at the Funding Institution**.
  9. **Rule 9: Become a Reviewer Early in Your Career** — Observing panel dynamics firsthand reveals how proposals are actually championed or sunk in room discussions.
  10. **Rule 10: Accept Rejection and Deal with It Appropriately** — Address every reviewer critique systematically and dispassionately without defensive hostility.

### 4.4 中国国家自然科学基金 (NSFC) 官方研究属性分类与高阶人才/职称答辩指南

1. **国家自然科学基金委员会 (NSFC) 官方分类评审框架** — *《研究属性》与四类科学问题属性定位*:
   - **Verified Official URLs**:
     - NSFC Official Research Attributes Portal: https://www.nsfc.gov.cn/p1/2961/2962/4089/yjsx.html
     - Official Historical Four-Category Specification (Archived University Science & Tech Office Notice): https://kjc.shnu.edu.cn/ef/4a/c16762a716618/page.htm
   - **2024+ Reformed Two-Category Research Attribute System (两类研究属性分类评审 — 适用于面上项目、青年科学基金项目[C类]、重点项目等)**:
     1. **自由探索类基础研究 (Curiosity-Driven / Free-Exploration Basic Research)**: Aimed at expanding scientific frontiers, proposing original hypotheses/theories, or discovering new phenomena/mechanisms through autonomous academic exploration.
     2. **目标导向类基础研究 (Goal-Oriented / Mission-Driven Basic Research)**: Aimed at resolving fundamental scientific bottlenecks behind major national strategic needs, industrial core technologies (*“卡脖子”难题*), or socio-economic challenges.
   - **Classic Four Scientific Problem Attributes (四类科学问题属性 — Foundational Positioning Logic for NSFC Written & Oral Defenses)**:
     1. **鼓励探索、突出原创 (Original / Zero-to-One)**: Ideas emerging from scratch (*“从 0 到 1”*), challenging existing paradigms with brand-new scientific questions or directions.
     2. **聚焦前沿、独辟蹊径 (Frontier / Unique Approach)**: Tackling hot/difficult international frontier bottlenecks via novel concepts, unique hypotheses, or unconventional methodologies.
     3. **需求牵引、突破瓶颈 (Demand-Driven / Bottleneck-Breaking)**: Extracting the true underlying scientific problem from urgent national or engineering application bottlenecks and breaking through critical limits.
     4. **共性导向、交叉融通 (Commonality-Oriented / Interdisciplinary Integration)**: Extracting shared foundational problems across disciplines and solving them via deep cross-disciplinary fusion.

2. **秦四清研究员 (Prof. Qin Siqing, 中国科学院地质与地球物理研究所 / IGGCAS，科学网精选长文)** — *《职称答辩：成果在硬不在多》* (Applicable to NSFC Talent Defenses [杰青/优青/青A/青B], Major Project Reviews & Senior Academic Promotion Defenses):
   - **Verified URL**: https://blog.sciencenet.cn/blog-575926-1561553.html
   - **Core Reviewer Mindset**: *"答辩不是成果清点，而是学术身份的确认"* (A high-stakes talent/promotion defense is NOT an inventory count of papers and grants — it is the confirmation of your distinct scholarly identity). Seasoned review panelists silently ask **three decisive questions**:
     1. **问题真不真 (Is the scientific problem real and unavoidable?)**
     2. **证据硬不硬 (Is the evidence rock-solid?)**
     3. **这件事有没有不可替代性 (Is your contribution irreplaceable?)**
   - **Three-Tier Depth Chain (三层深度递进)**:
     1. **Tier 1 — Real Unavoidable Problem (绕不过去的真问题)**: Not chasing trendy buzzwords or artificially slicing papers, but locking onto a bottleneck the discipline cannot bypass.
     2. **Tier 2 — Hard Verifiable Evidence (硬证据)**: Testable, reproducible, traceable results with **explicitly calibrated error bars and validity boundaries (带标定误差与适用边界)**.
     3. **Tier 3 — External Real-World / Disciplinary Impact (外部影响)**: Adoption into engineering codes/standards (进规范), industrial software (进软件), real engineering decisions (进工程决策), or independent follow-up verification by peers (同行跟进).
   - **Anti-"Paper-Catalog" Three-Part Slide Architecture (反对“论文目录投影式 PPT”的三段提纯结构)**:
     - **Opening (~30 seconds)**: State crisply who you are scientifically — what class of problem you solve, by what core methodology, and under what conditions your solution holds.
     - **Middle Core (3 Representative Evidence Blocks)**: Present at most **three flagship evidence blocks** following the strict visual rule: **“一页一图，图上有量纲、有对照、有边界”** (*One slide, one core figure — with physical units/dimensions, baseline comparisons, and explicit operating boundaries on the figure*). Replace mechanical *"Background $\rightarrow$ Method $\rightarrow$ Result $\rightarrow$ Conclusion"* templates with a closed problem-solving arc: **what the problem was $\rightarrow$ what paths were tried $\rightarrow$ where conventional paths failed $\rightarrow$ where the key conceptual pivot occurred $\rightarrow$ how many independent validations were run $\rightarrow$ what remains unsolved**.
     - **Closing**: Concrete impact + **where your research goes over the next 5 years and what exact bottleneck currently blocks it**.
     - **Appendix Rule**: **“其余全部进附录，附录不怕厚，正文必须瘦”** (*Move all secondary papers, exhaustive tables, and extra proofs into backup appendix slides — the appendix can be as thick as needed, while the main deck must stay lean*).
   - **High-Stakes Q&A & Attribution Iron Rules**:
     - When challenged on limitations: **first acknowledge the exact boundary honestly, then state the validated domain of applicability, and finally cite backup evidence**;
     - **Strict academic integrity in role attribution**: Never inflate *"participated in"* (参与) into *"led/chaired"* (主持), and replace hollow self-praise phrases to which panelists are completely immune (such as *"国际领先"* / *"world-leading"*) with concrete quantitative benchmarks.

3. **Verified Open-Source NSFC Proposal Repositories**:
   - **`fylimas/nsfc`**: https://github.com/fylimas/nsfc — Open-source NSFC application writing guide and structured LaTeX/Markdown workflow.
   - **`Ruzim/NSFC-application-template-latex`**: https://github.com/Ruzim/NSFC-application-template-latex — Unofficial NSFC Young/General program LaTeX template mirroring official outline sections.

## Part V — Academic Faculty Job Talks (高校教职面试学术报告 / 50-Min Job Talk)

### 5.1 MIT EECS Communication Lab — *Faculty Job Talk*

- **Author & Institution**: MIT Electrical Engineering & Computer Science (EECS) Communication Lab.
- **Verified URL**: https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/faculty-job-talk/
- **Dual Evaluation Mandate**: Unlike a conference paper talk (evaluated by narrow subfield peers on one paper), a faculty job talk evaluates the candidate simultaneously as a **researcher** (capable of leading an independent, fundable multi-year lab), a **teacher** (capable of explaining complex ideas clearly to undergraduates and out-of-area faculty), and a **future colleague**.
- **Build From Scratch**: MIT EECS explicitly warns candidates **not** to stitch together old conference decks; design the job talk from scratch around a unifying research identity.
- **The MIT EECS Hourglass Structure**:
  1. **Wide Top (Accessible Motivation & Upfront Contributions)**: Broad real-world/scientific motivation accessible to the entire department (e.g., theorists, systems builders, AI researchers, and hardware engineers alike), culminating in an **upfront executive overview of your thesis contributions and vision** within the first few minutes.
  2. **Narrow Waist (Technical Deep Dive)**: Deep technical formulation, core insights, architecture/proofs, and empirical validation on **1–2 flagship pieces of work** (proving genuine technical mastery to in-area specialists), with clear signposting when transitioning between levels of abstraction.
  3. **Wide Base (Broader Impact & 5–7 Year Pre-Tenure Research Vision)**: Zoom back out to broader cross-disciplinary connections and present a **concrete 5–7 year future research plan** (corresponding to the pre-tenure horizon: concrete next-step projects, longer-range moonshots, potential departmental collaborations, and funding/teaching synergies — optionally structured as a mini second hourglass).
- **The 75% Time Rule**: Allocate **at most 75% of the scheduled slot** to prepared speaking — i.e., **plan a `45-minute` talk for a `60-minute` seminar slot** to absorb clarifying interruptions during the talk and leave unhurried room for Q&A.
- **Q&A Tactics & MIT Exemplars**: Prepare curated backup slides for anticipated deep-dive questions; politely cap single-person monopolizers during mid-talk interruptions (*"Let's take the detailed derivation offline in our 1-on-1"*); distinguish clearly between *"I don't know"* (a gap in your own reading) and *"Nobody knows yet — that's an open research problem"*; study MIT EECS's cited real faculty job talk exemplars (Prof. Stefanie Mueller and Prof. Fadel Adib).

### 5.2 Shayna A. Sura et al. (*PLOS Computational Biology* 2019) — *Ten Simple Rules for Giving an Effective Academic Job Talk*

- **Authors & Venue**: Shayna A. Sura, Alyssa M. Smith, Katrina L. Ambrose, Clara E. Amorim, et al. (UCLA Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology), *PLOS Computational Biology*, Vol. 15, Issue 7, e1007163 (2019), DOI `10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007163`.
- **Verified URL**: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6657819/
- **Verbatim Ten Rules & Practical Mechanics**:
  1. **Rule 1: Know your audience** — Request an advance phone call with the search committee chair to learn who attends (faculty subfields, postdocs, graduate/undergraduate students), whether teaching ability is inferred heavily from the research talk, and room AV specifics.
  2. **Rule 2: Sell yourself** — Explicitly differentiate your intellectual trajectory from your PhD and postdoctoral advisors; subtly display productivity and fundability by placing concise **citation strings (highlighting your authorship in bold) and grant logos/acknowledgments along the bottom margin** of data slides.
  3. **Rule 3: Impress the in-crowd...** — Include **1–2 "muscle-flexing" slides** that showcase rigorous methodological/theoretical sophistication to domain experts on the faculty, with zero hand-waving or bluffing.
  4. **Rule 4: ...but also appeal to the out-crowd** — Every faculty member in the department typically gets a vote! Immediately follow deep technical segments with big-picture synthesis slides and recurring visual roadmaps so out-of-area colleagues stay engaged from start to finish.
  5. **Rule 5: Play the hand you've got to optimal effect** — Anchor the narrative on **2–3 cohesive studies** rather than rushing through everything you have ever published; use **1–2 conceptual synthesis diagram slides** to situate your broader publication portfolio, and close with both **immediate 2-year projects** (for your first PhD students) and a **5–10 year lab vision** aligned with realistic funding programs.
  6. **Rule 6: Give a good talk** — Target **45–50 minutes** for a 1-hour slot; remember faculty explicitly extrapolate *"Would this candidate teach our core undergrad/grad courses well?"* from slide clarity and pacing; carry both **native PPTX and flattened PDF backups** on USB plus cloud; eat a light snack and hydrate beforehand.
  7. **Rule 7: Be kind to your audience's eyes (plus Box 1 Visual Design Rules)** — Never paste dense multi-panel journal figures straight from a PDF: **redesign talk-specific simplified figures** with large axis labels, **progressively build complex multi-series plots** across click steps, enforce **colorblind-safe palettes paired with redundant line styles/point shapes**, and use consistent visual icons/silhouettes so listeners track experimental conditions effortlessly.
  8. **Rule 8: Embody the future** — Radiate genuine enthusiasm for where your research program is heading next, rather than delivering a retrospective museum tour of past thesis chapters.
  9. **Rule 9: Don't blow it in the Q&A session** — Listen fully without interrupting, briefly paraphrase complex questions for the room, pause to think, never bluff or get defensive, and keep **"spare home-run slides"** in the appendix to turn anticipated critiques into strengths.
  10. **Rule 10: Be professional** — Explicitly credit undergraduate/graduate mentees (with photos/names where appropriate) and collaborators to demonstrate mentorship track record while keeping your own intellectual leadership unmistakable.

### 5.3 Mark D. Hill, Patrick H. Winston, Matt Might & Michael D. Ernst — CS Faculty Job Talk Algorithms & Campus Visit Rules

1. **Mark D. Hill (Prof. Emeritus, UW-Madison CS)** — *7-Step Algorithm for Upgrading a 20-Min Conference Talk into a 60-Min Academic Interview Talk*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/conference-talk.html
   - **Hill's Published Time-Expansion Blueprint**:
     1. Start from your sharpest **20-minute conference talk**;
     2. Expand the 5-minute conference introduction into a **20-minute accessible opening** explaining the broad problem, why it matters, and the high-level intuition of your solution so every faculty member in the room follows the first third completely;
     3. Present the core technical conference talk body;
     4. Add **10 minutes of deeper thesis/technical material** in the middle to prove depth to specialists (*Hill notes it is acceptable if out-of-area listeners temporarily lose low-level details here, provided you warn them and bring them back*);
     5. Deliver an **accessible summary and future work section** that brings the entire room back onto the bus;
     6. Add a **10-minute breadth segment** surveying your other research projects (proving you are not a *"one-trick pony"*);
     7. Reserve **at least 5 minutes** inside the hour for Q&A.

2. **Patrick H. Winston (MIT CSAIL)** — *The 5-Minute Job Talk Rule*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/bc92763ffa0dad0ecafe44967e834e16_Unzc731iCUY.pdf (pp. 15–16)
   - Within the **first 5 minutes**, a job candidate must establish **(a) Vision** (a problem people care about + what is genuinely new in your approach) and **(b) Done Something** (enumerating the concrete steps required to realize that vision and showing you have already executed key steps). Close the job talk not with *"Thank You"*, but with a **Contributions slide** mirroring those steps.

3. **Matt Might (Prof. of Computer Science)** — *Advice for the Academic Job Hunt: "A good academic job talk"*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://matt.might.net/articles/advice-for-academic-job-hunt/
   - **Three-Tier Opening Defense**: Most CS faculty in the room are outside your subfield, so open with three concentric defenses (spending up to **10 minutes** keeping everyone on board):
     1. **Defend the host department's broad field** (why this area of computing matters);
     2. **Defend your subfield** (why its core problems are fundamental and timely);
     3. **Defend your specific work** (your thesis breakthrough).
   - **Depth vs. Breadth & Rehearsal Quota**: Cover **one vertical slice in genuine technical depth** while toggling between bite-sized formal/theoretical intuition and concrete empirical benchmark plots; **rehearse the full job talk 10 times** before your first campus interview and **2 times** before each subsequent campus visit; bring your own wireless presenter remote.

4. **Michael D. Ernst (Prof., UW Paul G. Allen School of CS & Engineering)** — *Getting an Academic Job: Scheduling & Delivering the Job Talk*:
   - **Verified URL**: https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/academic-job.html
   - **Campus-Visit Scheduling & Delivery Rules**:
     - Ask your host to schedule the job talk **early in the campus visit** (otherwise, in every 1-on-1 meeting before the talk, you waste 15 of your 30 minutes giving a mini-recap of your research) — **but NEVER at 9:00 AM**, when faculty attendance is sparse.
     - Explicitly request a **15–30 minute solo break in a private room right before the job talk** to rest your voice, drink water, review transitions, and test your laptop/dongles undisturbed.
     - **Hard Ceiling**: Never run past **50–55 minutes** even if told you have a 60- or 90-minute slot; keep water at the podium and carry redundant USB + web-hosted slide copies.
