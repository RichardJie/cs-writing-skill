#### B. 7 Shared Content Habits of Top-Rated Decks (`评分最高的 deck 共有的 7 个内容习惯`, verbatim extraction)
1. **Titles are conclusions, not topics (`标题是结论，不是话题`)**: e.g., `"同等算力下精度 +4.4 pp"` rather than `"实验结果"`.
2. **One idea per slide (`一页一个意思`)**: Top-rated decks (score 5) average **median 38 words/slide**, whereas low-rated decks (score $\le 3$) average **51 words/slide**—the excess words almost always come from copying paper paragraphs onto slides.
3. **One master figure carried throughout (`一张主图贯穿全场`)**: The system/framework diagram reappears across subsequent method slides with only the currently discussed module highlighted, instead of switching to a brand-new diagram every slide.
4. **Dedicated `"This work"` slide**: Right after motivation, summarize contributions in 1 slide (2–3 items), and echo the exact same contributions on the closing slide.
5. **Problem-driven method walkthrough (`问题驱动`)**: Unfold methods as `Problem → Insight → Design`, stating what problem each component solves before explaining how it works.
6. **Conclusion before chart on result slides (`结果页先说结论再给图`)**: Explicitly mark direction (`越低越好 ↓`), baselines, and key numbers so the speaker—not the audience—carries the chart-reading burden.
7. **Never end with only `"Thanks"` (`结尾不是“谢谢”`)**: The final slide carries takeaways / limitations / discussion prompts (`谢谢 + Q&A` may share that slide).

#### C. Upstream 18-Slide Group-Meeting Paper-Reading Spine (`1.1 组会读论文`, for presenting others' papers; brief 8–12 slides, detailed 16–22 slides)
> Core goal stated by author: **"让组里人在 15 分钟内知道这篇值不值得读、能不能借鉴"** (*Let labmates know within 15 minutes whether this paper is worth reading and what can be borrowed*).

| # | Slide Role | Required? | Question Answered by Slide (Upstream Contract) |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| 1 | Cover (`封面`) | ✓ | Original paper title + Chinese translation, venue/year, author affiliations, **presenter name & date separated from paper authors** |
| 2 | `Paper-at-a-glance` (`一页看懂`) | ✓ | Goal / bottleneck / 1-sentence method / 1 headline metric ($\le 25$ CN chars or 15 EN words each) + overview figure |
| 3 | Outline (`提纲`) | Optional | $\le 5$ items; omit for decks $\le 10$ slides |
| 4 | Background (`背景`) | ✓ | Minimal prerequisite concepts needed to follow the rest of the talk; 1 diagram instead of 3 paragraphs |
| 5 | Problem & Motivation (`问题与动机`) | ✓ | Concrete failure case, measurement, or cost number from the paper; title states `"现有方法在 X 下损失 Y%"` |
| 6 | Related Work Comparison (`相关工作对比`) | Optional | $2\text{–}3$ method families $\times$ capability matrix (`✓/✗`) or 2D map marking this paper's position (never a paper laundry list) |
| 7 | Core Idea / Contributions (`核心思路 / 贡献`) | ✓ | 2–3 verb-led 1-sentence contributions; acts as the anchor of the entire talk |
| 8 | Method Overview (`方法总览`) | ✓ | Framework figure ($\ge 50\%$ canvas) + `Input → Output` dataflow + novel modules highlighted |
| 9–11 | Method Details (`方法细节`, 1 module/slide) | ✓ | `What problem it solves → How it works → Why it works`; keep only 1 core equation with symbol annotations; pseudocode $\le 8$ lines |
| 12 | Experimental Setup (`实验设置`) | ✓ | Datasets (scale), baselines (why chosen), metrics (`↑/↓`), hardware/compute budget + 1-sentence fairness assessment |
| 13 | Main Results (`主结果`) | ✓ | Conclusion + headline delta in title; native editable pruned table ($\le 6 \times 8$, labeled `"节选自 Table N"`) or annotated figure crop |
| 14 | Ablation / Analysis (`消融 / 分析`) | ✓ (Detailed) | Which design matters most and which claimed contribution it proves |
| 15 | Qualitative Case / Running Example (`案例 / 可视化`) | Optional | 1–2 diagnostic examples (plus optional failure cases showing boundary); if paper centers on a concrete trace/problem, place a `Running example` slide *before* Method |
| 16 | Limitations & Presenter's Critique (`局限与我的看法`) | ✓ | Paper's self-reported limitations **explicitly separated** from presenter's independent observations |
| 17 | Lab Takeaways (`对我们组的启发`) | ✓ | What can be borrowed, whether reproducible, and connection to ongoing lab projects |
| 18 | Summary + Discussion Questions (`总结 + 讨论问题`) | ✓ | 3 single-sentence conclusions echoing Slide 7 + 2–3 concrete open discussion prompts |
| — | Backup Slides (`备份页`) | Optional | High-probability Q&A proofs, hyperparameters, complexity, extra comparisons placed after the final slide |

- **Brief (~10-slide) compression rule**: Keep Slides `1, 2, 5, 7, 8, 9 (merged into 1–2 slides), 13, 16+17 (merged), 18`.
- **Upstream Work Progress Report Spine (`1.5 工作汇报 / 项目进展`, 10–15 min)**:
  `封面 → 一页结论（上次以来最重要的 1–3 件事）→ 目标与计划回顾 → 进展（每项：做了什么、结果、和计划比）→ 问题与风险 → 下一步计划（带时间）→ 需要的支持 / 讨论` (all slide titles written as conclusions; ongoing tasks must state expected completion dates).

### 1.2 `HughYau/zuhui-skill` — Evidence-Backed Weekly Research Progress & Advisor Personality Routing
- **Source**: `https://github.com/HughYau/zuhui-skill` (Commit `dbf8e0b74a09ad7c2164ee94f7116e04c35f7630`, archived in `../upstream-skills/03-group-meeting-and-seminar/HughYau-zuhui-skill/`).
- **Core Principle (`SKILL.md` L12)**:
  > **“汇报的目标不是展示你有多忙，而是让对方迅速看懂四件事：做了什么、证据够不够、卡在哪里、是否需要决定或帮助。”**
  > (*The goal of a progress update is not to show how busy you were, but to let the recipient quickly grasp four things: what was done, whether the evidence is sufficient, where you are blocked, and whether a decision or help is needed.*)
- **4-Source Progress Pool (`Step 1: Build the progress pool`)**:
  1. **Git commits (`git log --since=<last_report_at>`)** + `git diff --stat` categorized into `experiment | feature | fix | docs | chore`.
  2. **Artifact directory scan (`artifacts.scan_dirs`)**: Timestamp-filtered figures (`*.png/*.jpg/*.pdf`), notebooks (`*.ipynb`), notes (`*.md`), and tables (`*.csv/*.tsv`), confirmed with the user before inclusion.
  3. **User verbal notes**: Paper reading, discussion conclusions, **discarded/failed attempts (`被否掉的尝试`)**, offline wet-lab/hardware runs.
  4. **Structured `progress_pool` schema**: Every item tracks `what`, `why`, `result`, `confidence` (`high | low | preliminary`), `evidence` (`git` SHA / `artifact` path), `asks`, `blockers` (`needs_help: bool`), `decisions_needed`, and `next_steps`.
- **4 Advisor Personality Presets (`references/advisor-presets.md`)**:
  - `hands-off` (*放养型*): Compress each item to 1–2 conclusion sentences; omit hyperparameters/intermediate steps; keep only `needs_help=true` blockers; **never omit `asks` and `decisions_needed`**.
  - `detail-oriented` (*刨根问底型*): Specify dataset, model, key hyperparameters, training epochs, mean $\pm$ std, baseline comparison, **failed experiments + hypothesized causes**, explicit `preliminary` tags, and presenter's preferred option for each `ask`.
  - `high-level` (*高屋建瓴型*): Lead with *"why we ran this"*, abstract metrics into hypothesis validation, link to overarching project goals, and front-load `decisions_needed`.
  - `intuitive` (*直觉比喻型*): Explain mechanisms in plain language/analogies while keeping headline numbers.
- **Bundled Weekly Beamer Slide Skeleton (`assets/templates/latex/slides.tex`, 16:9)**:
  `Frame 1: Title Page (Weekly Progress + Date Range)` $\rightarrow$ `Frame 2: This Week (headline empirical takeaways + figure)` $\rightarrow$ `Frame 3: Questions (decisions needed)` $\rightarrow$ `Frame 4: Blockers` $\rightarrow$ `Frame 5: Next Steps` (also ships matching Quarto `slides.qmd` and Typst `slides.typ` templates).

### 1.3 `Zero-stargazer/group-meeting-ppt-skill` — Duration-Aware Evidence Chain, Panel Cropping & Advisor Q&A Checklist
- **Source**: `https://github.com/Zero-stargazer/group-meeting-ppt-skill` (Commit `b39920c8e9795185f38894bf1c845a64d567e2c2`, MIT License, archived in `../upstream-skills/03-group-meeting-and-seminar/Zero-stargazer-group-meeting-ppt-skill/`).
- **Duration-Driven Evidence Chain (`skills/group-meeting-ppt-skill/SKILL.md` & `references/evidence-deck-spec.md`)**:
  - Rejects universal fixed page counts: every slide carries `estimated_seconds` summing to `meeting.target_seconds` (default 12-minute literature meeting = `660s` speaking budget).
  - Narrative arc: `research question → method / comparison → main evidence chain → conclusion → evidence boundary → discussion`.
  - **Strict Panel-Level Figure Cropping Gate (`references/release-gates.md`)**: Render pages at 300 DPI (`scripts/render_pdf_figure_assets.py`), materialize panel-level crops (`scripts/materialize_figure_crops.py`), and verify `crop_review` (`named_panel_matches`, `axes_and_legend_legible`, `article_prose_removed` — **never leave surrounding two-column paper prose or full caption paragraphs inside a figure crop**; put citation in the slide footer).
  - Density cap on figure slides: $\le 3$ evidence-backed bullets ($\le 58$ chars each) and `caveat` $\le 96$ chars so figures are never crowded out by commentary.
- **Upstream Pre-Meeting Checklists (`examples/sample-output/advisor_questions.md` & `integrity_checklist.md`)**:
  - **5 Categories of Likely Advisor Questions (`老师可能追问清单`)**:
    1. *Method Reliability (`方法可靠性`)*: Why is it reliable? What controls rule out coincidence? Does it hold under changed samples/conditions?
    2. *Data & Statistics (`数据与统计`)*: Is sample size sufficient? Are error bars, significance tests, and repeats clear? Are failure cases omitted?
    3. *Figure Interpretation (`图表解释`)*: Exactly which claim does this figure prove? What do axes, units, thresholds, and colors encode? Would the main claim survive without this figure?
    4. *Connection to Own Project (`和自己课题的关系`)*: Can we reuse the method, material, analysis pipeline, or presentation logic? What is the concrete Step-1 experiment if we borrow it?
    5. *Proactive Limitations (`你应该主动承认的局限`)*.
  - **Academic Integrity & Pre-Talk Self-Audit (`学术诚信与汇报自查清单`)**: Never fabricate numbers/claims absent from the PDF; cross-check sample size, units, $p$-values, concentrations, and conditions against the original text; distinguish **original paper facts** from **presenter interpretation**.

## Part II. Single-Paper Seminar & Journal Club Guides (论文精读组会与 Journal Club 权威学者/高校指南)

### 2.1 Srinivasan Keshav (University of Waterloo) — *How to Read a Paper* (*ACM SIGCOMM CCR*, 2007)
- **Source**: S. Keshav, *"How to Read a Paper"*, *ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review*, Vol. 37, No. 3, pp. 83–84, July 2007.
- **Verified URL**: `http://ccr.sigcomm.org/online/files/p83-keshavA.pdf`
- **Extracted Methodology for Seminar Preparation & Review**:
  1. **First Pass (5–10 minutes — Bird's-Eye Scan)**:
     - Read carefully: (1) Title, abstract, and introduction; (2) Section and sub-section headings (ignoring body text); (3) Conclusions; (4) Glance over references, ticking off ones already read.
     - **The Five Cs Answered After Pass 1**:
       - **Category**: What type of paper is this? (Measurement paper? Analysis of an existing system? Research prototype?)
       - **Context**: Which other papers is it related to? Which theoretical bases were used?
       - **Correctness**: Do the assumptions appear to be valid?
       - **Contributions**: What are the paper's main contributions?
       - **Clarity**: Is the paper well written?
  2. **Second Pass (Up to 1 hour — Grasping Content & Figures)**:
     - Read with greater care while skipping deep proofs; jot down key points in margins.
     - **Figure & Graph Audit**: *"Look carefully at the figures, diagrams and other illustrations in the paper. Pay special attention to graphs. Are the axes properly labeled? Are results shown with error bars, so that conclusions are statistically significant? Common mistakes like these will separate rushed, shoddy work from the truly excellent."*
     - Outcome: Able to summarize the main thrust of the paper, with supporting evidence, to someone else.
  3. **Third Pass (4–5 hours for beginners; ~1 hour for experienced readers — Virtual Re-Implementation)**:
     - *"Attempt to virtually re-implement the paper: that is, making the same assumptions as the authors, re-create the work. By comparing this re-creation with the actual paper, you can easily identify not only a paper's innovations, but also its hidden failings and assumptions."*
     - Identify and challenge every assumption in every statement; think about how you yourself would present a particular idea; jot down ideas for future work; pinpoint implicit assumptions, missing citations, and experimental/analytical issues.

### 2.2 Kayvon Fatahalian (Stanford University CS) — *Tips for Giving Clear Talks* (Nov 2023, 67 Slides)
- **Source**: Prof. Kayvon Fatahalian (Stanford Computer Science), *"Tips for Giving Clear Talks"*, Last update: November 2023.
- **Verified URL**: `https://graphics.stanford.edu/~kayvonf/misc/cleartalktips.pdf` (Companion systems-paper notes: `https://graphics.stanford.edu/~kayvonf/notes/systemspaper/`)
- **Verbatim Principles & 12-Tip Checklist**:
  - **Two Good Reasons vs. Two Bad Reasons to Give a Talk (Slides 4–5)**:
    - *Good*: (1) **Helping others**: *"The goal is not to tell the room what you did. Tell the room the most important things you think they should know, but probably do not, because they haven't spent as much time as you on your specific research problem."* (2) **Helping yourself**: *"get feedback from others to advance your own research goals (Goal: put smart people in the best position to help you)."*
    - *Bad*: Treating a talk as a reward for finishing a project, OR *"You were signed up to speak in a research lab meeting or in CS197. A good attitude: presenting is an opportunity, not an obligation."*
  - **Tip 1 — Identify your audience & strive for clarity (Slides 11–12)**: Even experts (your advisor or lab peers) haven't been thinking about your problem 60 hours a week. Recite sentences out loud to test whether someone outside your daily project would understand them.
  - **Tip 2 — "Every sentence matters" & Framing Intros/Related Work (Slides 13–24)**:
    - Cut generic platitudes (e.g., *"LLMs have been shown to have good task performance..."*) and generic outline slides (*"Never ever, ever, ever do this! This slide just told me this talk will have an introduction and a conclusion."*).
    - *"Unlike a paper, related work in a talk does not exist for academic completeness. No one cares if citations are comprehensive... The goal of the intro and background is to tell the listener: 'During this talk, here is the way I want you to think about the problem I am trying to solve.'"*
  - **Tip 3 — Set up the problem: establish inputs, outputs, and constraints early (Slides 25–27)**: *"Given these inputs, we wish to generate these outputs... We are working under the following constraints."* Understanding whether a solution is "good" requires knowing these goals and constraints upfront.
  - **Tip 4 — Show, don't tell (Slides 28–36)**: Words are ambiguous; show concrete examples of input data and target output directly (*"This is what the input looks like!"*).
  - **Tip 5 — The audience prefers not to think (much) (Slides 37–39)**: Listeners have a finite supply of mental effort. Do not make them decode figures, infer assumptions, or guess *why* you are telling them something; save their mental energy for evaluating limitations, implications, and connections to their own work.
  - **Tip 6 — Surprises in narrative are almost always bad: Give the *why* before the *what* (Slides 40–42)**: Always state where you are going and why before diving into an algorithm step or evaluation metric (*anticipation* test, credited to Abhinav Gupta: if you cannot anticipate what comes on your next slide without notes, restructure the narrative).
  - **Tip 7 — Always, always, always explain any figure or graph (Slides 43–47)**:
    - Explain every visual element and explicit highlight color (*"Sample points falling within the triangle are colored red"*).
    - Use *"As you can see..."* as "verbal eye contact."
    - **3-Step Results Graph Voiceover**: (1) Anticipate the question the graph addresses $\rightarrow$ (2) Explicitly describe the axes (which must have labels!) and direction ("Faster code is to the right") $\rightarrow$ (3) State the one takeaway point.
  - **Tip 8 — In the results section: One point per slide! And the point is the title of the slide! (Slides 48–53)**: Audience members read graphs to *verify* the stated point, not to *discover* it. Remake paper graphs to remove curves/data unrelated to that slide's point (noted by Fatahalian as essential for conference talk polish, optional for informal talks).
  - **Tip 9 — Slide titles matter (Thumbnail Test, Slides 54–56)**: Reading only your slide titles in thumbnail view from start to finish should yield a complete summary of the talk.
  - **Tip 10 — Stage your talk with colloquial section slides (Slides 57–59)**: e.g., *"I'm about to frame the problem in my terms"* $\rightarrow$ *"Ok, let's talk about our solution"* $\rightarrow$ *"Let's take a look at how well it works"* $\rightarrow$ *"Of course there are some potential alternatives that we didn't go with"* $\rightarrow$ *"Let me wrap up"*. Gives lost listeners a re-entry point.
  - **Tip 11 — End on a positive, big-picture intellectual note (Slides 60–62)**: Avoid boring laundry-list future work (*"we will apply this work to X"*); reflect on what the work means for the broader field.
  - **Tip 12 — When receiving feedback on a practice talk, the audience is always right (Slides 63–64)**: Never flip back to a slide defensively saying *"I mentioned that here."* Instead ask: *"I tried to explain that idea here, and this is what I was trying to say. What could I have said to make that point more clear?"*

### 2.3 Celia M. Elliott (UIUC Department of Physics, PHYS 596) — *Template for a Journal Club Presentation*
- **Source**: Celia M. Elliott, *"Template for a Journal Club Presentation"*, Department of Physics, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, PHYS 596, Oct 27, 2017.
- **Verified URL**: `https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys596/fa2017/Lectures/JCTemplate_596_Fall2017.pdf`
- **Extracted Slide Budget, Spine & Layout Rules**:
  - **Strict Time-to-Slide Math (Slide 7)**:
    $$\text{25 min total} - \text{5 min Q\&A} = \text{20 min talk} \;\div\; \text{2 min/slide} = \mathbf{10\text{ slides max}} \quad (\text{Title} + \mathbf{8\text{ Body Slides}} + \text{Summary})$$
    *(Allow more than 2 min/slide for dense slides, equations, or tabular data.)*
  - **Exact 8-Slide Body Breakdown (Slide 7)**:
    1. `Problem / motivation`
    2. `Background — what audience needs to know (prior work)`
    3. `What is new`
    4. `Why it is significant`
    5. `Methods`
    6. `Results`
    7. `Conclusions`
    8. `Your critique of the paper` (strengths, weaknesses, likely impact)
  - **Anti-Generic Outline Rule & Running Footer Tracker (Slides 2–6)**: Generic `Outline` slides (*"Background, Methods, Results..."*) are *"vastly overrated—little meaningful content, eminently forgettable."* If an outline is used, make it content-rich (specific physics/concepts), and place a **running outline bar at the bottom or left margin** of every slide highlighting the active topic (e.g., `Theory • InSb Nanowires • 0-energy Peaks • MF Observed • Applications • Critique • Q & A`), paired with a left-justified full-sentence motivating statement at the top.
  - **Final Slide Exposure Law (Slides 8–9)**: Never end on a pointless `"QUESTIONS?"` slide. *"The last slide will get the longest audience exposure—make it count!"* Keep a substantive bulleted Summary recap + presenter contact info on screen throughout Q&A.

### 2.4 Mark D. Schwartz et al. (NYU School of Medicine / UPenn) — *Improving Journal Club Presentations (<10 Minutes)*
- **Source**: Mark D. Schwartz, Deborah Dowell, Jaclyn Aperi & Adina L. Kalet, *"Improving journal club presentations, or, I can present that paper in under 10 minutes"*, *Evidence-Based Medicine*, 2007;12(3):66–68 (`doi:10.1136/ebm.12.3.66-a`).
- **Verified URL**: `https://www.med.upenn.edu/mdresearchopps/assets/user-content/Resources/ImprovingJournalClubPresentations.pdf`
- **Session Format**: **10-minute lean presentation + 20-minute group discussion + 1-page summary handout** (modeled on clinical Critically Appraised Topics / CATs).
- **Verbatim 10-Step Presentation Guideline**:
  1. **Describe the case or problem that attracted you to this paper** (real scenario hook).
  2. **Explain how you came across this article** (brief search strategy / query).
  3. **3A. Describe the study descriptors** (question type, study design, multicenter/setting, distinguishing features) **+ 3B. State the Research Question (PICO: Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparison/Control, Outcome)**.
  4. **State the importance / relevance / context of this question** (1–3 sentences on prior literature gap).
  5. **Describe the methods by giving concrete detail on PICO components** (inclusion/exclusion, dosing/protocol, follow-up duration, primary & secondary endpoints).
  6. **State answers to critical appraisal questions on internal validity** (randomization, allocation concealment, intention-to-treat, baseline balance, blinding, follow-up completeness; evaluate *direction and magnitude* of suspected bias—*"Statistics are a tool while study methods rule!"*).
  7. **Summarise the primary results** (lead listeners' eyes to the specific table row or graph bar; translate relative vs. absolute effect sizes / NNT; *"The conclusions giveth but the methods taketh away!"*).
  8. **Assess external validity** (why results can or cannot be applied to your own setting/population).
  9. **Conclude with your own decision about utility—resolve the opening case/question**.
  10. **Prepare a 1-page summary of the outline above as a storable handout**.

### 2.5 Alec Jacobson (Univ. of Toronto) & Colin Raffel (UNC) — *Role-Playing Paper-Reading Seminars* (2021)
- **Source**: Alec Jacobson & Colin Raffel, *"Role-Playing Paper-Reading Seminars"*, March 17, 2021 (tested across Columbia 2015, UofT 2019–2020, UNC 2020).
- **Verified URL**: `https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~jacobson/images/role-playing-paper-reading-seminars.pdf`
- **Diagnosis of Standard One-to-Many Seminar Failure**: Standard single-presenter paper summaries cause non-presenting students to disengage or ask vacuous universal questions (*"How efficient is this method?"*), while presenters devolve into shallow linear summaries void of critical analysis.
- **Verbatim 7 Rotating Paper-Reading Roles**:
  1. **Scientific Peer Reviewer**: Pretend the paper is an unpublished submission to the field's top venue (e.g., NeurIPS, SIGGRAPH); complete a full official review form and justify Accept/Reject + required revisions.
  2. **Archaeologist**: Treat the paper as unearthed in the desert; find and report on **one older cited paper** that substantially influenced it and **one newer citing paper** that built upon it.
  3. **Academic Researcher**: Propose an imaginary follow-up research project made possible *only* by the existence and success of the current paper.
  4. **Industry Practitioner**: Pitch why a specific company/product should pay you to implement this paper's method, analyzing at least one positive and one negative real-world application impact.
  5. **Hacker**: Rapidly implement a simplified/toy version of the core algorithm (e.g., 2D instead of 3D, or toy dataset) from scratch (not just running authors' repo) and live-demo code + results at the session midpoint.
  6. **Private Investigator**: Research the academic background and prior trajectory of one of the paper's authors (where they studied/worked, what prior projects led to this problem).
  7. **Social Impact Assessor**: Audit the paper's self-assessed societal impact and identify overlooked positive and negative societal consequences.
- **Shared Slide Protocol**: Instead of isolated full slide decks, participants drop visual aids into **one shared Google Slides deck**, titling each slide `[role emoji]: [student name]`, preceded by a rapid-fire ice-breaker where every attendee pitches an alternative paper title or missing experiment.

### 2.6 Harvard SEAS (Elena Glassman & Nada Amin), UW (Michael Ernst), Stanford (Christopher Potts), UNT/VT (Ian Parberry), UIUC (Matthew J. Miller), McGill, PLOS & Springer Nature

1. **Harvard CS 252r & CS 279r — Elena Glassman & Nada Amin (`https://pl-hci-seminar.seas.harvard.edu/How-to-present-279r.pdf`)**:
   - **Design Argument Decomposition**: Extract *Stakeholders + Domain* (*"Person P in setting S wants goal G, blocked by obstacles $O_{1..N}$ under constraints $X_{1..N}$"*), *Need Thesis* (how existing approaches fail + evidence), and *Approach Thesis* (*"Our approach has characteristics $C_{1..N}$..."* + what was borrowed from analogous settings + what differentiates it from failed prior solutions + empirical evidence).
   - **5-Step Seminar Workflow & Time Split**: (1) Annotate paper by design arguments $\rightarrow$ (2) Fill out the 8-pack summary $\rightarrow$ (3) Review peer forum questions beforehand to answer common/excellent ones $\rightarrow$ (4) Present design arguments, 8-pack, peer question answers, and a discussion prompt $\rightarrow$ (5) **Pacing**: **15–20 min presenting; 15–10 min discussion**.

2. **University of Washington CSE — Michael Ernst (`https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html`)**:
   - **Research Group Talk vs. Conference Talk vs. In-Class Paper Presentation**:
     - *Research group talk*: Goal is feedback to improve ongoing research; plan for a **highly interactive style with questions throughout** and ample discussion time at the end.
     - *Presenting someone else's paper (`In-class presentations`)*: *"You will be judged on how well other people understand the material at the end of the class, not on how well you understand the material at the beginning... Cover not only the technical details (people generally do a good job of this), but also **what is novel and why others didn't do it before**. That is just as important but very often overlooked. Focus on what is important about the paper, not just on what is easy to explain."*
   - **Concrete Slide Mechanics**:
     - Convince listeners of 3 things: problem is **worthwhile**, **hard**, and **solved**.
     - Pace: ~1 slide/minute; **never start with an outline slide** (use section outline slides with `⇒` + bold + color for talks $>30$ min); **never paste a raw PDF table**—reformat and prune non-essential columns/rows; **3 lines per bullet is always too much, 2 full lines is usually too much**; **zero pixel jitter** across build slides; keep the **Contributions/Conclusions slide** (never *"Thank you / Questions?"*) up during Q&A; distribute **numbered hardcopy slides** during practice talks (<10 people) for written per-slide annotations.

3. **Stanford CS224U — Christopher Potts (`https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224u/slides/cs224u-presenting-2023-handout.pdf`)**:
   - **Talk Arc (Slide 30)**: *Beginning* (What problem? Why important? What was tried & why incomplete?) $\rightarrow$ *Middle* (What data? What model/representations? How evaluated?) $\rightarrow$ *End* (Quantitative graphs; which components contributed most; **concrete error examples of what we still get wrong**; overall what happened & why).
   - **Stuart Shieber's *Rational Reconstruction* vs. *Historical Style* (Slide 12)**: Reject *Historical Style* (*"a whole history of false starts, wrong attempts, near misses"*) and *Continental Style* (unmotivated solution dump); present an idealized *Rational Reconstruction* that motivates every step cleanly.
   - **Geoff Pullum's Six Golden Rules (Slide 31, citing `http://www.lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/goldenrules.html`)**: (1) Don't ever begin with an apology; (2) Don't underestimate audience intelligence; (3) Respect time limits; (4) Don't survey the whole damn field; (5) You're an advocate, not the defendant; (6) Expect questions that will floor you.
   - **Two Valid Slide Design Schools (Slides 34–38)**: *Minimalist* (spare slides, fast transitions, best for short story-driven paper talks) vs. *Comparative* (dense but clear chalkboard-like slides kept up long to establish multiple cross-comparisons, best for teaching/seminars).

   - **4-Part Top-Down Theory Paper Template**: (1) *Introduction* (<5 min problem definition, motivation, minimal terminology, tabular synopsis of prior work, explicit contributions, content preview) $\rightarrow$ (2) *Body* (formal theorem statements without full proofs, link back to informal intuition, high-level proof structure sketch) $\rightarrow$ (3) *Technicalities* (pick **just ONE key non-trivial lemma** to show what happens under the hood; cut this section first if short on time) $\rightarrow$ (4) *Conclusion* (hindsight synopsis + weaknesses & open problems to defuse hostile Q&A).      - `Merits and shortcomings` (independent critique of strengths & flaws): **20%**

### 3.2 Marco Pautasso (CNRS France) — *Ten Simple Rules for Writing a Literature Review* (*PLOS Comput Biol*, 2013)
- **Source**: M. Pautasso, *"Ten Simple Rules for Writing a Literature Review"*, *PLOS Computational Biology*, 9(7): e1003149, 2013 (`doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003149`).
- **Verified URL**: `https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003149`
- **Key Structural Rules Applicable to Literature-Survey Group Meetings**:
  - **Rule 2 (Replicable Triage)**: Track exact search queries/databases, define explicit **inclusion/exclusion criteria** upfront, and inspect prior reviews first to identify a fresh angle and post-review developments.
  - **Rule 4 (Descriptive vs. Integrative vs. Systematic)**: Distinguish *descriptive* summaries (study-by-study methods/findings) from *integrative* synthesis (extracting shared concepts/mechanisms) and *systematic/meta-analytic* protocol-driven evidence tests.
  - **Rule 6 ("Reviewing the literature is not stamp collecting")**: A survey must critically identify methodological problems and deliver three clear takeaways to the audience:
    1. **The major achievements** in the reviewed field;
    2. **The main areas of debate** (conflicting findings/assumptions);
    3. **The outstanding research questions / gaps**.
  - **Rule 7 (Conceptual Scheme Diagram, Not IMRaD)**: Standard research-paper IMRaD (`Intro/Methods/Results/Discussion`) fails for surveys. Instead, build a **conceptual scheme / mind-map / taxonomy diagram** to organize and visually anchor how sub-topics link together, bookended by context and take-home messages.
  - **Rule 9 & Rule 10**: Situate your own lab's relevant work objectively (neither PR brochure nor self-denial), and pair cutting-edge preprints/papers-in-press with foundational older studies (*"sleeping beauties"*).

### 3.3 Jia-Bin Huang (`awesome-tips/related-work.md`) & S. Keshav (`How to Read a Paper` §3) — Anti-Laundry-List Taxonomy & 3-Step Survey Discovery
1. **Jia-Bin Huang — Grouping & Contrastive Framing (`https://github.com/jbhuang0604/awesome-tips/blob/main/related-work.md`, Commit `81b63ea398e561e95a89d511695bd3bbca797e8d`)**:
   - **Divide and Conquer by Topic**: Group prior work into distinct conceptual topics rather than an undifferentiated block.
   - **Trajectory + Relationship**: For each topic, present (1) the **trajectory** of research progress as a causal story (problem $\rightarrow$ why hard $\rightarrow$ how prior methods tackled it) and (2) the **explicit relationship** to your target problem/method (*"Unlike / In contrast to X, we..."*).
   - **Ban Laundry Lists**: *"No 'Author A did blah blah. Author B did blah blah. Author C...' Focus on the work, not the people."*
   - **One Key Contrastive Axis**: Distill clean conceptual dichotomies separating paradigms (e.g., `Multiple → Single`, `Content-agnostic → Content-aware`, `Static → Dynamic`), while remaining respectful (authors may be your reviewers/peers).
2. **S. Keshav — 3-Step Literature Survey Discovery (`http://ccr.sigcomm.org/online/files/p83-keshavA.pdf`, §3)**:
   - **Step 1**: Find 3–5 recent papers via Google Scholar; run **Pass 1** on each and read their *Related Work* sections (if a recent survey exists, start there).
   - **Step 2**: Extract **shared citations and repeated author names** across their bibliographies to pinpoint foundational papers, leading labs, and top conferences.
   - **Step 3**: Scan recent proceedings of those top conferences and run **Pass 2** across the combined core + recent paper set, iterating on newly discovered shared citations.

### 4.2 Jia-Bin Huang (`awesome-tips/working-with-mentor.md` & `steady-progress.md`) + Bill Freeman (MIT CSAIL, *How to Do Research*)
- **Sources & Verified URLs**:
  - Prof. Jia-Bin Huang (UMD / Virginia Tech / Meta), `https://github.com/jbhuang0604/awesome-tips/blob/main/working-with-mentor.md` & `steady-progress.md` (Commit `81b63ea398e561e95a89d511695bd3bbca797e8d`, MIT License).
  - Prof. William T. Freeman (MIT CSAIL), *"How to do research"*, March 6, 2013: `https://pengsida.net/files/Bill_Freeman_How_to_do_research.pdf` (cited directly in Huang's `working-with-mentor.md`).
- **Extracted Weekly Meeting & Progress-Slide Rules**:
  1. **Before / During / After Meeting Protocol (Huang, `working-with-mentor.md`)**:
     - *Before*: Send results and agenda ahead of time so mentors have time to digest them; **do not stay silent all week** if you got stuck 20 minutes after last week's meeting.
     - *In the meeting*: Give the progress update, and **reserve the last 10 minutes to discuss next steps**.
     - *After*: Send a concise written summary and an agreed actionable plan; stick to the agreed plan (or explicitly discuss why it should be revised).
  2. **The "One Single Rolling Slide Deck" Law (Huang, `working-with-mentor.md`, verbatim)**:
     > **"Put ALL the progress/results/figures/discussions in one single slide deck. This saves 5 mins in the meeting locating files and trying to retrieve results two weeks ago when someone asks for it."**
  3. **Ban `"It doesn't work"` — Stepwise Isolation Diagnosis (Freeman, *How to do research* p. 1 & p. 3; quoted in Huang)**:
     > **Freeman verbatim**: *"Please don't tell me 'it doesn't work'. Of course it doesn't work. If there's a single mistake in the chain, the whole thing won't work... What I want to hear instead is something like: **'I've narrowed down the problem to step B. Until step A, you can see that it works, because you put in X and you get Y out, as we expect. You can see how it fails here at B. I've ruled out W and Z as the cause.'**"*
     - Freeman's valid forms of reportable weekly progress (p. 3): *"I've shown why this doesn't work"*, *"I've simplified the task to get it to start working"*, or *"I spent the whole time reading because I know I have to understand this before I can make any progress."*
  4. **Never Cancel Meetings When Stuck (Huang & Freeman)**: Canceling a meeting because you made little progress or hit a wall is *"a TERRIBLE idea"*—being stuck is the exact moment mentor feedback unblocks you.
  5. **Experimental Discipline for Weekly Progress (`steady-progress.md` & Freeman)**:
     - **Work backward** (`A → B → C`: assume oracle `B` first to test `C` and measure upper bounds early).
     - **Toy examples with full ground truth** (Freeman's `y = ab` scalar toy model for color constancy; Huang's synthetic ground-truth toy cases).
     - **Baseline first** & **Simple case first**.
     - **Change ONE thing at a time** in experiments, use fast **proxy** experiments for rapid iteration, **visualize everything** (inputs, intermediate activations, outputs—*"You cannot debug what you cannot see"*), and pair qualitative visuals with quantitative metrics.

### 4.3 Mingyu Gao / 高鸣宇 (Tsinghua University IIIS, *ACM SIGARCH Blog*) — Dual-Track Concise Slides + Offline Deep Docs
- **Source**: Prof. Mingyu Gao (IIIS, Tsinghua University), *"Having Effective Meetings Between Advisors and Students"*, *ACM SIGARCH Computer Architecture Today*, Nov 2, 2020.
- **Verified URL**: `https://www.sigarch.org/having-effective-meetings-between-advisors-and-students/`
- **Extracted Rules for 30–45 Minute Weekly / Bi-Weekly 1-on-1s**:
  1. **Kill Effort-Log Progress Reports**: Junior students often prepare long, detailed chronological logs of everything they did last week out of fear the advisor will think they worked too little. Gao argues this wastes scarce 30–45 minute meeting slots; instead, **briefly summarize where you stand in the overall roadmap, what blocks you, and your proposed next plan**, spending most of the meeting seeking forward advice.
  2. **Stage-Adaptive Focus**: *Early project stage* $\rightarrow$ establish overall research roadmap and expected key contributions; *Investigation stage* $\rightarrow$ prioritize immediate next-week task ordering, handling negative results, and completeness checks.
  3. **Save Heavy Details to Offline Comprehensive Documents**: Keep meeting slides concise and high-level, while writing **separate, detailed offline technical documents** (theorem proofs, model specs, workflow descriptions, case studies) for the advisor to read before/after the meeting—which both exposes logical gaps early and can be directly reused in the final paper.
  4. **"Always Measure One Level Deeper" (`Zoom in to the next level of details`)**: Before reporting an unexpected result at a meeting, drill down one level of granularity (e.g., from aggregate packet-latency tail $\rightarrow$ individual tail packets $\rightarrow$ route patterns $\rightarrow$ individual hotspot links) so you bring diagnostic evidence rather than raw confusion.

### 5.3 Zhihu High-Upvote Academic PPT Tutorial (`知乎高赞《学术展示系列：学术PPT教程（上）外观基础 +（下）内容讲解》`)
- **Verified URLs**:

- **Extracted Visual, Chart, Formula & Literature-Slide Rules**:
  1. **Color & Background (Part I §1–§2, pp. 3–9)**:
     - Prefer **pure white background (`纯白底`)**—classroom/seminar projectors lack studio lighting, making dark backgrounds wash out or glare.

Avoid curved decorative split templates, blurred background photos, or watermarks that collide with content.
     - **Mandatory Slide Numbers (`页码是必须放的，方便交流`)**: Include date + slide number above a thin bottom rule so advisors/peers can pinpoint exact slides during Q&A.
  2. **Alignment & Spacing (Part I §2.2 & §6, pp. 10–11, 26–27)**:
